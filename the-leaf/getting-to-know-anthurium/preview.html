Genus Basics: The Anthurium

The largest genus in the arum family is also its most misunderstood, and its foliage rewards the patient far more than its plasticky red bloom ever suggested.

Say the word Anthurium to most people and they picture the flamingo flower: a glossy, lipstick-red heart with a yellow tail, sold in supermarket floral departments and forgotten on office windowsills. That plant is real, and it is an Anthurium. It is also the least interesting thing the genus does. The red heart is not even a flower in the way you think, and it accounts for a rounding error among the roughly one thousand named species climbing, creeping, and hanging through the wet forests of the American tropics.

What collectors chase is the leaf. Anthurium is the connoisseur's genus among houseplants, generally slower to grow, fussier about air and water, and more dependent on humidity than the Philodendrons it is so often confused with. The reward for that extra work is foliage with the texture of crushed velvet, of corrugated metal, of hammered pewter veined in silver. Once you have seen a good one in person, the supermarket heart stops looking like the same plant at all.

Common questions

How do I tell an Anthurium from a Philodendron?
Look for the geniculum, a swollen bendable joint, a little knee, at the top of the leaf stalk where it meets the blade; Anthurium has it and Philodendron does not. The second tell is new growth: Anthurium wraps each leaf in a persistent papery prophyll, while Philodendron sheds disposable cataphylls that yellow and fall away.
Which Anthurium should a beginner buy first?
Start with Anthurium clarinervium. It delivers the signature velvet leaf and white veins while tolerating the humidity and watering mistakes that would quickly kill demanding species like warocqueanum. Grow it well for a season and you will have learned the care routine the whole genus shares.
Why do the leaves get permanent silver scarring?
That damage is almost always thrips, tiny sap-sucking insects that rasp the leaf surface. On velvet-leaved species the scarring never heals, and because Anthurium keeps its leaves for years, the marks stay visible and can even carry into new growth. Inspect new leaves constantly and quarantine every incoming plant.
Is the red flamingo flower a real Anthurium?
Yes, it is a genuine Anthurium, usually a hybrid bred for its showy spathe. But what looks like a flower is really an inflorescence: the central spike, the spadix, carries hundreds of tiny true flowers, and the red heart behind it is a modified leaf, the spathe. The foliage species most collectors prize have far less conspicuous blooms.
