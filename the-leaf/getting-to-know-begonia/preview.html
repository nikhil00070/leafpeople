Genus Basics: The Begonia

Two thousand species, one crooked leaf, and a foliage habit that turns cautious houseplant owners into obsessive collectors.

Pick up almost any begonia and look at a single leaf head-on. One side of the blade is bigger than the other where it meets the stalk, as if the plant were drawn by someone who lost interest halfway across. That crooked, uneven shape is the whole family's signature, and once you can see it you can name a begonia across a greenhouse, a windowsill, or a photo taken by a stranger in a rainforest.

Here is the thing worth saying up front: begonias are not aroids. They belong to their own family, Begoniaceae, and the genus Begonia is one of the largest groups of flowering plants on the planet, somewhere near 2,000 species. Leaf People files them next to the philodendrons and anthuriums for one honest reason, which is that the same people tend to collect both. But a begonia and a monstera are about as related as a house cat and a heron.

Common questions

How do I know if a plant is actually a begonia?
Look at a single leaf where the stalk meets the blade. If the two halves of the leaf are visibly unequal, one side reaching lower than the other, it is almost certainly a begonia. This lopsided "angel wing" shape holds across nearly the entire genus, no matter how different the plants look otherwise.
Are begonias aroids like philodendrons and monsteras?
No. Begonias belong to their own family, Begoniaceae, and are only distantly related to aroids. Collectors keep them alongside aroids because the same people tend to grow both, but botanically they are entirely separate groups. The genus Begonia is one of the largest of all flowering plant genera, with roughly 2,000 species.
Why does my begonia keep rotting at the base?
Crown rot comes from water sitting on the base of the plant, especially in humid, still air. Water the soil directly and keep the crown and leaves dry, use a fast-draining mix, and make sure air actually moves around the plant. Humidity is fine for rex and jewel types as long as it is paired with airflow.
Which begonia should a beginner buy first?
Start with a cane or angel-wing type such as Begonia maculata, the polka-dot begonia. These tolerate normal room humidity and occasional missed waterings far better than the demanding rex cultivars, and they grow quickly enough to feel rewarding. Once you have their care down, the fussier foliage types are much less intimidating.
