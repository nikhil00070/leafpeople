Rainforest: The Daintree, Australia — The Oldest Rainforest on Earth

At Cape Tribulation, two World Heritage areas meet and 180 million years of botanical memory press against the sea.

The road north of the Daintree River ferry crossing narrows almost immediately, and the forest closes over it like a hand. Palms lean across the bitumen. Roots have buckled the shoulders of the road into something closer to suggestion than surface. By the time you reach Cape Tribulation — where the forest runs directly into the Coral Sea and two UNESCO World Heritage areas share a shoreline — the ordinary sense of being in a living landscape has shifted into something harder to name. You are walking through deep time. Not metaphorically. The lineages overhead predate the continents as we drew them.

The Wet Tropics of Queensland, inscribed as a World Heritage Area in 1988, contains what is widely described as the oldest continuously surviving tropical rainforest on Earth — somewhere between 135 and 180 million years old, older than the Amazon by a long margin, a fragment of the forested supercontinent Gondwana that somehow outlasted the geological violence that remade everything else. The Daintree is its heart. For a collector who grows Rhaphidophora or trails Hoya australis across a trellis in a spare bedroom, standing here is not tourism. It is provenance research, conducted in the field.

Common questions

Can you visit the Daintree rainforest to see aroids growing in the wild?
Yes. The Cape Tribulation section of Daintree National Park is accessible by road, and native climbing aroids including Rhaphidophora australasica and Pothos longipes are visible on forest walks. Boardwalk trails near Cape Tribulation and Mossman Gorge offer close access to the understorey without leaving marked paths, which is both a legal and ecological requirement in the World Heritage Area.
Is Hoya australis easy to grow indoors?
Hoya australis is among the more adaptable hoyas in cultivation — it tolerates a wider humidity and temperature range than many tropical species. It prefers a well-draining, bark-based substrate, bright indirect light, and a drying period between waterings. As an epiphyte it is sensitive to consistently wet roots, so a dense potting mix or a pot without drainage will cause problems faster than neglect will.
Why does the Daintree have so many ancient plant families?
The Wet Tropics of Queensland provided a climatically stable refuge — consistent rainfall, warmth, and a closed canopy — across an extraordinarily long timeframe, allowing lineages that were eliminated elsewhere by drought, cold, or geological disruption to persist. The region's isolation on the Australian continent as it drifted north added to this refugial effect, concentrating primitive angiosperm families with nowhere else to go.
What substrate do wild climbing aroids like Rhaphidophora australasica grow in?
In the Daintree, Rhaphidophora australasica grows on the bark of rainforest trees, with roots exploring accumulated organic debris, moss, and bark crevices rather than mineral soil. The substrate is airy, moisture-retentive after rain, and fast-draining between events. This is why chunky bark-based mixes or mounted growing replicates their natural conditions better than conventional potting soil.
