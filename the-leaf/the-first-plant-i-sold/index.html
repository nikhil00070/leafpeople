The First Rare Plant I Sold, and What It Taught Me About Pricing

A single rooted cutting of a velvet philodendron, a kitchen scale, and the slow education of a hobbyist learning what scarcity is actually worth.

The first rare plant I ever sold was a two-node cutting of Philodendron verrucosum, the Ecuadorian form with the bristled red petioles and the deep emerald blades that look black until the sun catches them. It was rooted in a deli cup of sphagnum, two new leaves the size of guitar picks, and I had no idea what to charge for it. I remember weighing it on the kitchen scale like that would tell me something. It weighed 38 grams. I wrote the listing three times.

I settled on $85, which felt obscene at the time and now reads, six years later, like a small act of self-sabotage. The buyer was a woman in Vermont who messaged me twice to confirm the cutting would survive the cold pack. It did. She sent a photo of the first leaf to harden off in her grow tent — already bigger than anything on the mother plant — and I sat with my coffee and understood, dimly, that I had just learned the first real lesson of the trade: I had priced a plant, not a relationship, not a risk, not a future.

What the cutting actually was

The mother had come to me as a sad import, one of a flat of verrucosum that a small importer in Florida had brought in under CITES paperwork from a nursery near Mindo. Half the flat melted. Mine kept one leaf, threw a cataphyll within a month, and by the following spring was climbing a cedar plank with leaves that ran nine, then eleven, then fourteen inches. I took the cutting from the second node up — clean stem, two aerial roots already pushing, a node fat enough to promise a strong break.

People who have never sold a plant think the cutting is the product. It isn't. The product is the genetics behind it, the acclimation work that turned a half-dead import into a thriving specimen, the fact that the mother had proven itself in cultivation outside a cloud forest. A node from a plant that has survived a year in someone's apartment is worth more than a node from a freshly landed import, even if they came off the same wild collection. The market doesn't always price this correctly. Collectors who have lost a $300 import to crown rot start to.

I didn't know any of this when I listed it. I thought I was selling 38 grams of leaf and root.

You are not selling a plant. You are selling the next two years of someone's grow tent, and the gamble that your cutting will be the one that takes.

The three prices in every listing

Every rare plant has three prices stacked inside it, and the seller's job is to know which one they are quoting. There is the replacement price — what it would cost the seller to acquire the same genetics again, including the failures along the way. There is the market price — what comparable cuttings are moving for on the platforms that week. And there is the relationship price — what this particular buyer, at this particular moment, with this particular grow setup, is willing to pay.

The $85 I charged was a market price, and not even a current one. I had pulled it from a half-remembered sold listing on a Facebook group from months earlier. The replacement price, had the mother died that winter, would have been closer to $400 — the cost of another import flat and the statistical likelihood of losing most of it. The relationship price, for a woman with a humidified tent and the patience to message twice about cold packs, was probably $150 and she would have paid it without flinching.

Pricing is not arithmetic. It is the practiced reading of which of the three prices the moment calls for.

Scarcity is not what you think it is

The word rare gets used badly. A plant is not rare because it is hard to find on a Tuesday in Ohio. It is rare because of some combination of slow growth, difficult propagation, narrow native range, regulatory friction, and the bottleneck of how many serious growers are willing to maintain the mother stock. Anthurium dressleri is rare in the proper sense — slow, finicky, geographically pinned to a slice of Panamanian forest, easy to hybridize accidentally and lose the true line. Philodendron 'Pink Princess' was never rare; it was briefly fashionable, and tissue culture caught up within eighteen months.

The collector market punishes sellers who confuse the two. Price a fashionable plant like a rare one and you will be holding stock when the TC labs flood the shelves. Price a genuinely rare plant like a fashionable one and you will sell out in an afternoon to flippers, watch your genetics disperse into ten mediocre grow rooms, and never see a clean cutting of it again.

I sold my verrucosum to a real grower by accident. The next one I sold — and I remember this clearly — went to someone who listed it again two weeks later for $220. That was the second lesson.

The flipper problem

You cannot fully prevent flipping, but you can shape it. The tools are small and a little fussy. Asking buyers what their setup is. Selling unrooted wet sticks to people who have proven they can root them, and rooted plants to people who haven't. Pricing close enough to the real market that the arbitrage isn't worth the shipping risk. Building a list of repeat buyers who get first refusal before anything goes public.

None of this is moralizing. Flippers serve a function — they move stock around and surface price information. But if you are a small grower of, say, Anthurium papillilaminum from a known clean line, and you watch your cuttings end up cross-pollinated with hybrids two seasons later, you stop selling to strangers. The genetics matter more than the margin.

The collectors I respect most are the ones who under-price to the right people on purpose, and refuse to sell to the wrong ones at any number.

What the buyer is actually buying

Here is the thing I wish someone had told me before I weighed that deli cup. A buyer of a rare aroid cutting is buying, in descending order of importance: the genetics, the acclimation, the seller's willingness to answer a question in March when something goes wrong, the packaging, and finally the plant material itself. The leaves on the cutting are almost irrelevant. They will yellow off in transit or in the first month of adjustment. What matters is the node and the story behind it.

This is why the same cutting from two different sellers can fairly be priced at $90 and $250. The $250 seller is including, whether they articulate it or not, two years of email support and the implicit promise that if the cutting fails through no fault of the buyer, something will be made right. The $90 seller is selling a node, full stop. Both are legitimate. Confusing them is where sellers get hurt.

I price now with the support included. I lose some sales to cheaper listings. I almost never have a buyer come back angry.

The kitchen scale, in retrospect

I still have the scale. It sits on a shelf in the propagation room next to the perlite and the bottle of KelpMax, and I use it occasionally to weigh sphagnum for a sub. I have not weighed a cutting on it since that first sale. The instinct to quantify the plant by its mass was the instinct of someone who did not yet understand what they were selling — someone who thought a node was a commodity and a price was a calculation.

A price is a sentence you write about a plant and the person you are sending it to. Sometimes the sentence is short and the number is low because you want the cutting to go somewhere it will be loved into a specimen. Sometimes the sentence is long and the number is high because you have done real work, and the work deserves to be paid for, and the buyer who balks at the number is not your buyer.

The verrucosum in Vermont is, I'm told, now a six-foot climber on a slab of tree fern, throwing leaves the length of a forearm. The woman sends me a photo every spring. I have never raised the price on her for anything since.

Rare plants, real stories — a few times a week.
