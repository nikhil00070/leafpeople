Botanical Tour: The Amazon Spheres, Seattle, USA — A Collector's Cathedral in the City

Three interlocking domes dropped into downtown Seattle hold one of the most ambitious living plant collections in the United States — and it doubles as someone's office.

Step off Lenora Street in Seattle's Denny Triangle neighborhood and you are suddenly looking at something that has no business being there: three tessellated glass spheres, roughly the size of small cathedrals, fused together and rising out of the sidewalk like a fever dream of Victorian greenhouse architecture landed in the twenty-first century. At night, the warm amber glow coming through the hexagonal panes makes the whole structure look lit from within by something living. Which, of course, it is.

The Amazon Spheres opened in January 2018 as part of Amazon's sprawling downtown headquarters campus. Architecture firm NBBJ designed them, but calling these domes a corporate amenity is like calling a Monstera deliciosa a houseplant — technically accurate, entirely inadequate. Inside is a cloud-forest conservatory holding on the order of 40,000 plants drawn from dozens of countries, with a dedicated horticulture team that grows, rotates, and curates the collection from an off-site greenhouse. It is, without much exaggeration, the kind of place serious plant collectors describe when they try to explain to non-collectors what the obsession is actually about.

Why a Tech Company Built a Rainforest

The premise behind the Spheres was, at its core, about workspace psychology: that people think differently when surrounded by plants, and that a genuinely biodiverse environment is more restorative than a potted Dracaena in a conference room. Amazon's horticulture team, brought on specifically to develop and steward the collection, leaned into that premise with unusual seriousness. The emphasis was not on tropicals that happen to survive indoors, but on species native to cloud-forest elevations — plants adapted to high humidity, bright diffuse light, and the kind of steady cool warmth that is, frankly, expensive to maintain.

NBBJ's design had to reconcile two things that fight each other: a glass structure that maximizes light for plants and a habitable workspace for people who need regulated temperatures and acoustics. The result is an interior that reads as humid and lush without becoming oppressive — temperatures are kept in a range that supports both cloud-forest species and human comfort. The steel-and-glass geometry of the dome itself, with its distinctive pentagonal and hexagonal panes, manages solar gain while letting in the diffuse Pacific Northwest light that actually suits epiphytes reasonably well.

What distinguishes the Spheres from a well-budgeted hotel atrium is the commitment to genuinely difficult plants. The collection leans heavily on epiphytes — plants that grow on other plants without parasitizing them, colonizing bark, branches, and wire frames the way they would a mossy tree limb at two thousand meters elevation. Orchids, bromeliads, ferns, and aroids make up large portions of the interior planting, many of them mounted or suspended rather than potted in soil.

A living wall of epiphytes and rare aroids is not an amenity. It is an argument — for what a building can be.

Rubi and the Architecture of the Big Plants

The single most discussed resident of the Spheres is a Ficus rubiginosa the team calls Rubi. The tree stands roughly fifty-five feet tall and was craned into the structure whole during construction, before the final dome panels closed the building around it. Rubi is not a specimen you walk past — it occupies a central vertical axis of the space, and the birdnest structure, a large sculptural installation of woven branches that serves as a meeting area, wraps around and near it. The combination of massive fig and sculptural nest reads as something between a treehouse and a cathedral crossing.

Craning a mature tree into a building before sealing the roof is not a casual decision. It signals the degree to which the botanical program was baked into the architecture from the start rather than planted in afterward. The off-site greenhouse that supports the Spheres exists precisely because a collection this size, with this level of species specificity, requires plants at different stages of development, in rotation, being conditioned for interior life before they ever go on display. That is the same logic a serious collector uses when they acclimate a new Anthurium warocqueanum in a humidity tent before moving it to open shelving.

The four-story living green wall is the other feature that stops people in their tracks. Planted in a modular system that covers an interior wall from near floor to upper canopy level, it incorporates hundreds of species in vertical layers — the kind of installation that, in a private collection, would be the crowning achievement of a decade of growing. Here it is also a backdrop for a meeting room.

The Epiphyte Logic

For collectors of aroids and orchids, the Spheres' planting philosophy is immediately legible. Cloud-forest epiphytes are the stars of the collection because cloud-forest epiphytes are the stars of the serious hobby right now, and for overlapping reasons: they are difficult, species-rich, often stunning, and genuinely threatened in habitat. Genera like Anthurium, Philodendron, and Monstera appear here not as the domesticated forms most people recognize but as species representatives — plants with the leaf texture, petiole geometry, and growth habit of their wild relatives.

Orchids in the Spheres are similarly treated as botanical subjects rather than florist props. Mounted on cork or bark, allowed to sprawl in the way epiphytic orchids actually grow, they reinforce the sense that this is a collection assembled with some taxonomic intention. The horticulture team has been documented attending to rare and endangered species, which is the part that matters most if you care about where the hobby intersects with conservation. A plant that is genuinely scarce in the wild, growing in a maintained collection inside a workplace, is carrying more than aesthetic weight.

The birdnest structure — the woven sculptural canopy near Rubi — is also heavily planted with epiphytes along its surfaces. Moss, ferns, small orchids, and bromeliads colonize it the way they would colonize a fallen log in a cloud forest. It is one of those design gestures that takes a genuinely long time to fully register: you think you are looking at a piece of furniture, and then you realize you are looking at a microhabitat.

What the Visit Actually Looks Like

The Spheres are a private workplace with limited public access, and it is worth being direct about that. Amazon has offered public visiting hours on select Saturdays, with advance registration required and capacity restrictions in place, but the schedule changes and prospective visitors should check Amazon's official channels before making plans around a visit. The experience on public days is not the same as working there — you are moving through a curated path rather than spending a full morning in the canopy-level meeting areas — but the living wall, Rubi, and the epiphyte installations are all accessible.

The honest collector's response to the Spheres is a mixture of genuine awe and mild productive envy. The awe is straightforward: this is a biosphere dropped into a city downtown, maintained to a standard that most public botanical gardens would struggle to match, with a plant list that overlaps significantly with the wishlists of anyone who follows rare-aroid culture closely. The envy is equally straightforward: the budget, the team, and the square footage required to do this are not available to anyone without a market capitalization in the trillions.

What it offers to a visitor who grows plants seriously is something a photograph cannot: the three-dimensional reality of how these species actually occupy space when given room. A Philodendron allowed to climb a proper structure, a Monstera with leaves at a scale its genes always intended, orchids at eye level that you normally see only in the top canopy of field photographs — these are calibration experiences. They recalibrate the eye for what a plant is trying to become.

The Collection as Argument

There is a version of the Amazon Spheres story that is purely about corporate branding, and it is not wrong exactly, but it is not the most interesting version. The more interesting version is that someone had to actually specify the plants, source the epiphytes, design the substrate systems, maintain the off-site greenhouse, hire the horticulturists, and rotate the collection with the logic of a curatorial program — and all of that happened, and continues to happen, inside a functional office building in a city of 750,000 people.

The Spheres are not a botanical garden in the institutional sense. They have no public research program, no herbarium, no education department open to walk-ins. But they are doing something that serious botanical gardens do: keeping alive species that are under pressure in habitat, doing so with expertise and intention, and making the argument — by sheer existence — that biodiverse plant collections belong in the built environment at a scale most architects never consider.

For collectors who spend their weekends in humidity tents arguing about substrate ratios and chasing down tissue-culture drops of rare Anthurium species, the Spheres are a useful landmark. They show what the logic of the serious plant collection looks like when it is given real resources. The plants are the same plants. The care principles are the same principles. The scale is simply — extravagantly, almost absurdly — different.

Common questions

Are the Amazon Spheres open to the public?
The Spheres are primarily a private Amazon employee workspace. Amazon has offered limited public visiting hours on select Saturdays, with free timed entry passes required in advance. Availability and scheduling change regularly, so check Amazon's official Spheres page before planning a visit.
What plants are inside the Amazon Spheres?
The collection holds on the order of 40,000 plants representing hundreds of species, with a strong emphasis on cloud-forest epiphytes including orchids, bromeliads, ferns, and aroids from genera such as Philodendron, Anthurium, and Monstera. A four-story living green wall and a roughly 55-foot Ficus rubiginosa named Rubi are the signature features.
Who designed the Amazon Spheres?
The Spheres were designed by architecture firm NBBJ and opened in January 2018 as part of Amazon's downtown Seattle headquarters campus. The botanical program was developed in parallel with the architecture, including the off-site greenhouse that supports the ongoing collection.
What should I not miss on a visit to the Amazon Spheres?
The four-story living green wall, the tree called Rubi (Ficus rubiginosa, craned in whole before the dome was sealed), and the birdnest structure colonized with epiphytes are the three features most worth orienting your visit around. The epiphyte-mounted orchids and aroids throughout the interior are also worth studying closely if you grow these plants at home.
