Botanical Tour: Berlin's Großes Tropenhaus, Germany — One of the Tallest Glass Jungles on Earth

In Dahlem, a steel-and-glass vault built in 1907 holds a full-canopy jungle 26 metres above a Berlin footpath.

Step inside on a grey November morning and the shift is immediate: the cold drops away, the air thickens with moisture, and somewhere overhead — genuinely overhead, four or five storeys up — a canopy of palms closes against the glass. This is the Großes Tropenhaus, the Great Tropical House at the Botanischer Garten Berlin, and the first thing it does is make you recalibrate your sense of scale. Most glasshouses are long and low, a suggestion of the tropics pressed flat under a pane. This one is a vault, an interior sky, and the plants inside it have had a century to believe it.

The garden sits in Dahlem, in the southwest of the city, spreading across roughly 43 hectares of ground that hold something in the order of 20,000 plant species — one of the largest collections on earth by any measure. The Großes Tropenhaus is its architectural and botanical centrepiece, and it was built to do something specific: to let the tropics grow tall.

Steel, Glass, and the Logic of Height

The Großes Tropenhaus was constructed between 1905 and 1907, part of a larger glasshouse complex designed when the Botanischer Garten relocated from its earlier site in the Schöneberg district to the roomier grounds of Dahlem. The move was an act of ambition: the old garden had run out of space, and the new one was conceived at a scale that would let Berlin compete with Kew, with Leiden, with the great botanical institutions of the colonial botanical world. The architect Alfred Koerner oversaw the glasshouse design, and the central nave of the Tropenhaus reaches approximately 25 to 26 metres at its peak — a figure that sounds like an engineering boast until you stand beneath it and understand that it is, first of all, a horticultural decision.

Height in a tropical house is not decoration. A glasshouse that tops out at eight or ten metres can grow palms, but it will spend decades pruning them back, bending their crowns, negotiating with their natural ambition. The Tropenhaus simply does not have that problem. Roystonea and Livistona palms rise through the middle stories unchecked. Fig trees reach the upper glass. The internal climate stratifies the way a real forest does: humid and shadowed at ground level, brighter and somewhat drier toward the canopy. That gradient is not incidental — it is what allows the garden to grow both the deep-shade understorey plants and the high-light canopy species in a single structure, within a single city block, at 52 degrees north latitude.

The steel framework that holds all this glass in place was state-of-the-art for its era, and the building has been comprehensively restored. A major restoration completed between 2006 and 2009 addressed the structure's steel and glazing without dismantling what matters most: the old trees. Those palms and figs and bamboos were carefully maintained through the works, and when the house reopened it was, in the best possible sense, exactly as it had been — just sound again.

A glasshouse that reaches 25 or 26 metres is not a greenhouse. It is a room-sized argument that the equator can be moved.

A Century of Growing Into It

What the 1905-1907 construction started, a century of continuous cultivation finished. The oldest specimens in the Tropenhaus have had more than a hundred years to establish root systems, to layer bark, to reach upward in the unhurried way of tropical trees that have never been told the season is changing. The result is a planting that looks less like a botanic display and more like a fragment of actual forest — dense, layered, slightly chaotic in the way that living systems are.

Berlin's botanical history is long and scientifically serious. The institution behind the garden, the Botanischer Garten und Botanisches Museum Berlin, maintains one of the world's major herbaria, holding millions of dried specimens and functioning as a research centre for plant systematics. That scientific weight matters for a collector visiting the Tropenhaus: the plants are labelled with authority, the taxonomy is current, and the curators are not decorators. When you find an aroid climbing a trunk in the understorey and the label reads Rhaphidophora or Epipremnum, you can trust it.

The garden survived the Second World War with damage, and the collections were rebuilt over the postwar decades with deliberate care. The Tropenhaus today represents not just the original 1907 vision but the accumulated decisions of generations of curators — which species to acquire, which climbers to let run, which specimens to protect and propagate.

What the Vertical Space Allows

For a collector of aroids, the practical wonder of the Tropenhaus is exactly what it offers the plants: vertical structure. In nature, the species that serious hobbyists chase — velvet-leaved Philodendron, climbing Anthurium, the various Monstera beyond the familiar deliciosa — are not groundcover. They are climbers and hemi-epiphytes that spend their lives moving upward, their leaves changing form as they ascend, their root systems half in bark and half in air. A low glasshouse defeats them. It turns a vigorous climber into a sprawling mess, forces reversions to juvenile leaf form, denies the plant the conditions under which its adult morphology develops.

The Tropenhaus gives its climbing aroids something to climb, and enough height that the climb is meaningful. The moss-covered columns and the trunks of the large trees carry plants that have genuinely ascended — not two feet off the floor, but metres up, where light and humidity behave differently and adult leaf forms can express themselves. For a collector who has spent months trying to convince a Monstera or a Rhaphidophora to fenestrate properly, watching a specimen that has had decades and real height to work with is instructive in a way that no care guide fully communicates.

Bamboo in the Tropenhaus reaches the upper register of the vault, its culms leaning in the slight thermal drafts that move through the upper air. This is not a curiosity — it demonstrates the scale at which the structure operates. The building was built to contain things of this size.

The Understorey: Where Collectors Look

Ground level in the Tropenhaus is where a collector's attention tends to settle. The understorey planting is dense and changes across different zones of the house, moving between humid tropical forest and somewhat drier, more seasonal tropical conditions. Aroids appear throughout: the broad, patterned leaves of various Philodendron species, climbing stems of Epipremnum and related genera, the occasional Amorphophallus in season. These are not the rarest species in the world's trade, but seeing them grown to full maturity in conditions close to ideal recalibrates what you think the plants are capable of.

Ferns are another understorey strength. Large tree ferns — genera like Cyathea — stand at intermediate height between the ground plants and the canopy, their fronds unrolling in the diffuse light. The Botanischer Garten Berlin holds a significant cycad collection as well, plants that predate the flowering plants entirely, their stiff crowns occupying corners of the house with a prehistoric composure. Neither cycads nor tree ferns are what most aroid collectors grow, but encountering them at scale — old, large, healthy — is a reminder of how many evolutionary chapters are packed into a well-run tropical house.

Look at trunk surfaces and you will find epiphytes. Bromeliads sit in forks. Orchids have been established on bark in several areas of the complex. The aerial root systems of climbing aroids drape from heights that are easy to miss if you are looking only at the floor. The Tropenhaus rewards slow looking.

Beyond the Great House: Aquatic Plants and the Glasshouse Complex

The Tropenhaus is the centrepiece, but the broader glasshouse complex at Dahlem contains a series of smaller houses, each with a different climate regime and a different botanical focus. Among them, the aquatic house — containing the giant waterlilies of the genus Victoria — is the one most visitors remember longest. The immense circular pads, sometimes exceeding two metres in diameter in good growing conditions, float with a geometric improbability that familiarity with photographs does nothing to diminish in person. Victoria is a tropical plant, and keeping it in Berlin requires the controlled warmth of the aquatic house; the result is a spectacle with genuine scientific backstory.

The historic glasshouse complex as a whole dates to the original Dahlem construction period, and its range of structures — different widths, different heights, different orientates to light — reflects a pre-modernist approach to climate management in which architecture itself was the primary tool. There were no sophisticated electronic climate controls in 1907; the shape, orientation, and thermal mass of the glass structures did the work. Walking through the complex in sequence is a minor education in nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century botanical engineering.

For collectors interested in orchids, the orchid house warrants time. The collection is not focused on cut-flower hybrids; the Botanischer Garten grows species orchids, many of them small and ecologically specific, the kind of plants that reward close attention and a willingness to kneel on cool stone to read a label.

Why Northern Europe Changes Everything

The argument the Tropenhaus makes is a geographic one, and it is most legible in winter. Berlin is a cold-continental city. January temperatures fall well below freezing. Snow sits on the Dahlem streets. Inside the Tropenhaus, none of that is happening: the air is warm and saturated, a palm is dropping a dead frond somewhere above you with a sound like a slow exhale, and Philodendron leaves the size of a dinner tray are collecting condensation in their midribs. The contrast between the city outside and the forest inside is not a trick. It is the point.

For anyone who grows tropical aroids in a temperate home, the Tropenhaus is an argument in built form for what those plants need and what they can become. The collector who coaxes a Philodendron gloriosum across a moss pole in a spare room, monitoring humidity and cursing the central heating, is working toward a distant version of what is simply happening in Dahlem — passively, continuously, in the same glass building it has been happening in since the reign of Wilhelm II.

There is something clarifying about this. The plants are not difficult because they are exotic or fragile. They are difficult because they are deeply, specifically adapted to conditions that northern Europe does not provide naturally. The Tropenhaus provides them artificially, at enormous ongoing cost and care, and the plants respond by growing as they were always going to grow given the chance. That is the lesson, and it is worth the train to Dahlem to receive it.

Planning a Visit

The Botanischer Garten Berlin is open year-round, with seasonal variation in hours; checking the official garden website before visiting is advisable since hours shift considerably between summer and winter. The garden and glasshouses charge a modest admission fee. The full glasshouse complex, including the Tropenhaus and the aquatic house, is included in the standard entry.

From central Berlin, the garden is accessible by S-Bahn to Botanischer Garten station or by U-Bahn to Rathaus Steglitz with a short onward journey. Allow at least two hours for the glasshouse complex alone; serious plant people consistently find that half a day disappears without difficulty.

The ideal visiting season for most of the glasshouses is any time, since the tropical collection is constant. For the giant waterlilies, summer and early autumn are the productive season when Victoria pads reach their full size. For the contrast of jungle against winter city — the experience that most directly makes the case for what the Tropenhaus is — a visit between November and February is hard to beat.

Common questions

Where is the Botanischer Garten Berlin and how do I get there?
The Botanischer Garten is located in the Dahlem district of southwestern Berlin. It is reachable by S-Bahn to Botanischer Garten station or by U-Bahn toward Rathaus Steglitz. The main entrance is on Königin-Luise-Straße.
Is the Großes Tropenhaus open to the public?
Yes, the Großes Tropenhaus is open to visitors as part of the standard glasshouse complex included in the garden's general admission. Hours vary by season, so confirming current opening times on the garden's official website before visiting is recommended.
What makes the Großes Tropenhaus architecturally significant?
The Tropenhaus, built 1905–1907 and restored 2006–2009, reaches approximately 25 to 26 metres at its peak, making it one of the tallest glasshouses in the world. Its height allows tropical trees, palms, and bamboo to grow to near-natural stature, creating a genuinely multi-storey tropical forest inside a temperate European city.
What plants should I look for as an aroid or epiphyte collector?
The understorey of the Tropenhaus holds climbing aroids including Philodendron and Epipremnum species grown to full maturity on large tree trunks. The complex also includes orchid species, tree ferns, cycads, and the aquatic house with giant Victoria waterlilies. Labels are maintained to current botanical standards.
