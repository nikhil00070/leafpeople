Variegation on the Edges: The Cream-Margined Carnosa and Its Mirror-Image Sibling

Two hoyas, one species, and a naming muddle that has frustrated collectors for decades — here's how to tell them apart and why it matters.

Walk into any serious hoya collection and you will almost certainly find a cream-margined Hoya carnosa somewhere near the window, its waxy leaves edged in ivory or blush pink, new growth flushing an improbable coral before hardening to green. It is not a rare plant. It has been in cultivation since at least the mid-twentieth century, sold under half a dozen names depending on which decade you bought it, and yet collectors still argue about it in online forums with the intensity usually reserved for authenticated Hoya dressleri or a confirmed Borneo-provenance lacunosa. The source of that argument is almost always the same: someone has confused their 'Krimson Queen' with their 'Krimson Princess,' or bought one expecting the other, and now they are staring at a rooted cutting that does not match the photo in the listing.

The confusion is understandable once you know the architecture of variegation involved. These two cultivars are essentially photographic negatives of each other, and both go by names that include the word 'Krimson,' which does not help. Understanding what each one actually is — how the color is distributed, what the new growth looks like, how stable the pattern runs, and which name belongs to which plant — is the kind of foundational knowledge that separates a collector from someone who just keeps plants alive.
