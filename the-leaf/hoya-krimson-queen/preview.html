Variegation on the Edges: The Cream-Margined Carnosa and Its Mirror-Image Sibling

Two hoyas, one species, and a naming muddle that has frustrated collectors for decades — here's how to tell them apart and why it matters.

Walk into any serious hoya collection and you will almost certainly find a cream-margined Hoya carnosa somewhere near the window, its waxy leaves edged in ivory or blush pink, new growth flushing an improbable coral before hardening to green. It is not a rare plant. It has been in cultivation since at least the mid-twentieth century, sold under half a dozen names depending on which decade you bought it, and yet collectors still argue about it in online forums with the intensity usually reserved for authenticated Hoya dressleri or a confirmed Borneo-provenance lacunosa. The source of that argument is almost always the same: someone has confused their 'Krimson Queen' with their 'Krimson Princess,' or bought one expecting the other, and now they are staring at a rooted cutting that does not match the photo in the listing.

The confusion is understandable once you know the architecture of variegation involved. These two cultivars are essentially photographic negatives of each other, and both go by names that include the word 'Krimson,' which does not help. Understanding what each one actually is — how the color is distributed, what the new growth looks like, how stable the pattern runs, and which name belongs to which plant — is the kind of foundational knowledge that separates a collector from someone who just keeps plants alive.

The Base Plant: What Hoya carnosa Actually Is

Hoya carnosa is a climbing epiphyte native to eastern Asia — China, Japan, India, and across into parts of Southeast Asia — and it has been in Western cultivation long enough to accumulate an ungainly number of common names. It grows vigorously on bark or wire frames, produces the characteristic umbellate clusters of star-shaped flowers that smell faintly of vanilla in the evening, and tolerates conditions that would collapse more finicky species. The leaves are succulent enough to store a little water, thick enough to feel almost rubbery, and covered in a waxy cuticle that gives the genus its popular name. In its straight species form, the leaves are a solid, dark, glossy green — handsome but not especially dramatic.

Variegation in carnosa occurs along a fairly predictable axis: color is lost either from the margins inward or from the center outward, producing two distinct patterns that have been selected for, named, and in some cases renamed so many times that the historical record reads like a minor clerical disaster. The plants themselves are stable enough in the right conditions, but the naming conventions around them are not, and this is where collectors begin to suffer.

Both variegated forms entered the US trade in the latter half of the twentieth century under the 'Krimson' branding, which was apparently a marketing decision by a commercial grower rather than a botanical designation. Neither name appears in any formal taxonomic literature. They are trade names, full stop, and trade names in horticulture are notoriously inconsistent across vendors, regions, and decades. Knowing this does not resolve the confusion, but it does explain why expecting the labels to be reliable is optimistic.

A cream margin and a cream center sound like minor variations until you realize the plant's ability to photosynthesize depends entirely on which one you've got.
