What 'aff.' and 'sp.' Really Mean on an Anthurium Label

The small italics on a nursery tag are not decoration — they are a taxonomist's confession, and every serious anthurium grower should learn to read them.

The tag came taped to a four-inch pot from a vendor in Florida: Anthurium aff. dressleri "Colón". I had paid more than I wanted to admit for it, and I stood in the kitchen turning the plant in my hands, reading those three letters again. Aff. A small word doing enormous work. It meant the seller was confident enough to invoke dressleri but honest enough not to swear to it. It meant the plant in my hands was something — and possibly something else.

Collectors talk about provenance and lineage and mother plants, but the real grammar of this hobby lives in the abbreviations stamped onto labels. sp., aff., cf., sp. nov., ined. These are not marketing flourishes. They are taxonomic shorthand, borrowed from herbarium sheets and field notebooks, and they tell you — if you know how to read them — exactly how much the person who wrote the label actually knows.

The vocabulary, briefly

sp. stands for species and means: this is an Anthurium, genus confirmed, species unknown or undetermined. It is the most honest label a collector can apply to a wild-collected plant whose identity has not been worked out. A label reading Anthurium sp. "Tarapoto" tells you the genus, the collection locality, and nothing else. That is not ignorance — it is precision about the limits of knowledge.

aff. is short for affinis, Latin for "related to" or "having affinity with". When a botanist writes Anthurium aff. dressleri, they are saying: this plant looks close to dressleri, shares meaningful characters with it, but differs in ways I cannot dismiss. It might be an undescribed sister species. It might be a population variant. It might, eventually, be folded into dressleri when someone publishes a revision. For now, it is affinis.

cf. is confer, "compare with". It is softer than aff. and is used when the writer suspects an identity but cannot confirm it from the material at hand. Anthurium cf. papillilaminum means: I think this is papillilaminum, but I cannot rule out something else without flowers, a mature leaf, or a better look at the petiole.

sp. nov. means species nova — a new species, not yet formally described. ined. (ineditus, unpublished) signals a name that is in use but has not appeared in a valid publication. Both are common in aroid circles, where the rate of discovery outpaces the rate of formal taxonomy by years, sometimes decades.

A label that says aff. dressleri is not hedging — it is the most honest thing a vendor can write when the type specimen is sitting in a herbarium in Panama.

Why the shorthand matters more in Anthurium than almost anywhere else

Anthurium is a genus of roughly a thousand described species and an unknown — possibly equal — number of undescribed ones. Tom Croat at the Missouri Botanical Garden has spent half a century working on it, and the backlog of unpublished material in his herbarium is, by his own admission, enormous. New species turn up faster than monographs can absorb them.

Then there is the matter of variation. A single species like Anthurium marmoratum ranges across cloud forests from Colombia into Ecuador, and the plants from one ridge can look strikingly different from those a valley away. Juvenile leaves rarely resemble adult ones. Heat-stressed leaves rarely resemble cool-grown ones. The pulvinus on a young crystallinum from Panama may not match the description in the 1905 protologue because the protologue described a different population entirely.

All of which is to say: when you see aff. on an anthurium label, the person writing it is operating in a genus where confident identification often requires a flowering specimen, locality data, and a working knowledge of the type material. Most of which is not in the room with the plant.

What an honest vendor is telling you

A label reading Anthurium aff. dressleri "Colón" is doing three jobs at once. It names the closest described relative — dressleri, a velvet species from Panama's Caribbean lowlands. It signals that the writer does not believe this plant is dressleri proper, perhaps because the leaves are narrower, the venation finer, the geographic origin different. And it preserves the locality, "Colón", which is the single most important piece of data attached to the plant.

Compare that to a tag that reads simply Anthurium dressleri on a plant of uncertain origin. The second tag is more confident and less useful. Confidence without provenance is how the hobby ends up with five different plants circulating under the same name, none of which match the type.

The collectors I trust most are the ones who use aff. and sp. freely. It is a marker of literacy in the genus. A vendor who labels everything to species, without hedging, without locality, is either working from extraordinary source material or — much more commonly — guessing.

The locality is the label

Notice that almost every careful anthurium tag ends in a place name in quotes: "Colón", "Chocó", "Tarapoto", "Esmeraldas", "Bajo Calima". These are not decorative. In a genus this poorly resolved, the locality often carries more identifying information than the species epithet.

Two plants labeled Anthurium sp. from different collectors are anonymous. Two plants labeled Anthurium sp. "Chocó" and Anthurium sp. "Tarapoto" are at least geographically distinguishable, and the geography correlates with morphology and growing conditions. A Chocó plant evolved in one of the wettest places on earth and will resent a dry winter in your living room. A Tarapoto plant came from a drier Andean foothill and tolerates more.

When provenance is lost, the plant becomes nearly impossible to place. This is why ex-situ collections at places like Marie Selby Botanical Gardens are so obsessive about accession data: a plant without a collection number is a plant without a past, and in Anthurium, the past is most of what we have to go on.

How names move from aff. to accepted

The path from aff. to a valid binomial runs through herbarium specimens, flowering material, and a published description in a journal that meets the rules of the International Code of Nomenclature. It is slow work. A plant might circulate in the trade as Anthurium sp. "Purple Velvet" for fifteen years before someone collects fertile material, compares it to existing types, and publishes a description.

Anthurium luxurians is a recent example of the inverse problem: a species long known in cultivation under various unofficial names, finally pinned down with a proper description and a type specimen. Anthurium dressleri itself was described by Croat in 1983, late by the standards of many genera, and plants that should have been called dressleri were circulating under other labels for years before.

Some aff. designations will eventually become their own species. Others will collapse into existing ones as taxonomists realize the variation is within the normal range of a polymorphic species. Either outcome is fine. What matters is that the label honestly reflected the state of knowledge at the time the plant changed hands.

What to do with the tag in your hand

Keep it. Write it down. If you repot the plant, transcribe the full label — aff., locality, vendor, date of acquisition — onto something more durable than a plastic stake that will fade in a year. I keep a small notebook for this, one line per plant, and the discipline has saved me more than once when a friend asks what that velvet anthurium in the back corner actually is.

Resist the urge to upgrade the label. If you bought it as Anthurium aff. dressleri, do not start calling it dressleri because the leaves are filling in and you are pleased with yourself. The whole point of the abbreviation is to preserve doubt where doubt exists. Strip the aff. and you have erased the most informative thing on the tag.

And when you propagate and pass cuttings along — which, if the plant thrives, you eventually will — pass the full label with them. The vendor in Florida wrote aff. dressleri "Colón" because someone before them wrote it on a collection bag in Panama. Keeping that chain intact is the small, unglamorous work that holds the hobby together.

Common questions

Is a plant labeled aff. less valuable than one labeled to species?
Not necessarily, and often the opposite. An aff. designation usually means the plant was identified by someone literate enough in the genus to recognize that it does not quite match the type, which is more informative than a confident but possibly wrong species name. For undescribed taxa, aff. plants can be more sought after because they may represent something new.
What does sp. nov. mean on an anthurium label?
Sp. nov. stands for species nova and indicates the plant is believed to be a new, undescribed species. It signals that a botanist or experienced collector thinks the plant warrants formal description but that the paper has not yet been published. Until publication, the name is provisional and cannot be cited as a valid binomial.
Should I correct an aff. label if my plant matures and looks like the species?
No — leave the label as you received it. The aff. reflects the identifier's judgment based on the original material, and morphology in mature cultivated plants can drift from wild forms in ways that mislead. If you genuinely believe the identification should be revised, note your reasoning separately rather than overwriting the provenance.
Why do anthurium tags include place names in quotes?
The locality is often more diagnostic than the species name in a genus this taxonomically unsettled. Plants from different populations can look distinct and require different care, and the locality preserves a link to the original collection that may become important if the species is later described or revised. Lose the locality and you lose most of the plant's history.
