Velvet Versus Bird's-Nest Anthuriums: Two Collectors, Two Worlds

The genus splits its devotees down the middle — one camp chases velvet and venation, the other chases scale and structure.

Marcos keeps his lights on a timer that clicks at 6:47 a.m., a few minutes before he comes downstairs to look at his Anthurium dressleri. The plant is mounted to a slab of tree fern in a converted shower stall in suburban New Jersey, and it has, by his count, produced fourteen leaves under his care, each one a shade closer to the bruised black-green he saw in a photograph from Cerro Jefe a decade ago. He talks about petioles the way other men talk about wine.

Across the country, in a warehouse outside Portland, Devon is up on a stepladder spraying down a six-foot Anthurium jenmanii with rainwater from a fifty-five-gallon drum. The leaves are leathery, paddle-shaped, arranged in a rosette wide enough to catch a falling cat. He has never owned a velvet anthurium and does not intend to. "They're jewelry," he says, not unkindly. "I grow furniture."

A genus that contains two religions

Anthurium is enormous — somewhere north of a thousand described species, with new ones still being formalized out of Ecuadorian and Colombian cloud forests every few years. Most hobbyists never encounter more than a dozen. But within the genus, two aesthetic and horticultural traditions have hardened into something close to rival schools.

On one side: the section Cardiolonchium and its neighbors, the velvets. A. clarinervium, A. crystallinum, A. magnificum, A. forgetii, A. warocqueanum, and the holy grail for many, A. dressleri. These plants are prized for the optical trick of their surface — epidermal cells shaped to scatter and trap light, producing that matte, sucking blackness against which silver veins glow. They are usually small to medium, slow, and unforgiving.

On the other side: the bird's-nest anthuriums, mostly section Pachyneurium. A. jenmanii, A. superbum, A. plowmanii, A. schlechtendalii, and the architectural A. salviniae. These are terrestrial or lithophytic, often colossal, with thick leathery foliage arranged in a vase that catches debris in habitat and feeds itself. They are, by anthurium standards, almost vulgar in their willingness to grow.

One camp grows light-trapping velvet at the scale of a hand; the other grows leathery rosettes at the scale of a small tree.

What the velvet collector is actually buying

A velvet anthurium is a study in restraint. The leaves are sometimes only six to fourteen inches long even on a mature plant, and the difference between a juvenile and an adult can take years to express. Collectors learn to read petioles — dressleri's are terete and quietly speckled, magnificum's are sharply four-winged, crystallinum's are rounded with a faint ridge — because at small sizes the leaves of half a dozen species look superficially alike.

Provenance matters here in a way it doesn't in most houseplant circles. A dressleri from a known Panamanian seed lot, traceable through one of the long-time growers who collected legally before export restrictions tightened, is a different object than a hybrid sold under the same name on a flipping page. The community knows the difference and polices it, sometimes nastily. Old-line growers like the late Dewey Fisk and the breeders working out of Hawaii and Florida cast long shadows.

The culture demands patience the plants enforce. Velvets sulk in the wrong substrate. They want airy mixes — bark, perlite, pumice, a little sphagnum — high humidity in the 70s, steady airflow, water that hasn't sat in tap pipes leaching copper. Get any of that wrong and they spite you with a brown halo on the next leaf, a record of the week you went on vacation.

What the bird's-nest grower is actually buying

The bird's-nest school is, in its own way, just as exacting — but the demands are spatial rather than temporal. A mature A. jenmanii 'wide form' will eat a square meter of floor. A flowering A. salviniae can throw a leaf longer than its grower is tall. People who collect these plants tend to have greenhouses, or sunrooms, or the kind of warehouse Devon rents, because nothing else works.

The aesthetic argument is structural. A good bird's-nest is sculpture: a tight central rosette, leaves emerging at the correct angle, ribs catching light like the vault of a cathedral. A. superbum's undersides are coppery; A. plowmanii ruffles its margins like a kale leaf gone monstrous; A. schlechtendalii can hold standing water at its core and grow epiphytically on a limestone outcrop. None of this reads in a photo the way a velvet does. You have to stand in front of one.

These plants tolerate things velvets won't. They take more direct light, lower humidity, drier substrate, and a benign neglect that would kill a warocqueanum in a week. The trade-off is space, and the patience of a different kind: a jenmanii from seed can take seven or eight years to look like the show plant you saw at an auction.

Two economies, one auction site

The market splits along the same line. Velvet anthuriums circulate in small, hot quantities — a single rooted cutting of a well-documented A. papillilaminum Ree clone might change hands for the price of a used car, then change hands again the following spring. Prices on hybrids like A. 'Dark Mama' or the Indonesian A. magnificum × crystallinum crosses peak and crash on a schedule that has more in common with sneaker drops than horticulture.

Bird's-nests move slower and cheaper, with a few exceptions. A common A. plowmanii from a box-store rescue is fifteen dollars; a well-grown specimen A. jenmanii 'Black' from a serious Thai grower is several hundred to a few thousand, depending on form. The premium goes to symmetry and leaf count, not rarity, because the species themselves are usually not particularly rare in cultivation — just hard to grow into something impressive.

What unites the two markets is a quiet, durable distrust of mislabeling. Both camps have been burned by seedlings sold as named clones, by hybrids passed off as species, by tissue-cultured stock whose parent plant was itself misidentified two transactions ago. The serious collectors keep notebooks. Some keep spreadsheets. A few keep DNA.

The crossover heretics

A small number of growers refuse to choose. They keep a wall of velvets under cabinet lights and a row of superbum under the skylight in the same room, and they talk about the genus as a continuum rather than a fork. These are often the most interesting collectors to visit, because they've thought about why the two factions exist and what each one is missing.

One grower I know in Quito keeps A. dressleri and A. salviniae within three meters of each other, because in habitat the elevations overlap and he likes the reminder. He'll point out that A. warocqueanum — the queen, the strap-leafed velvet that hangs four feet long on a mature plant — is technically itself a kind of architectural species, just one that happens to also catch light like a moth wing. The categories blur if you push on them.

That blurring is, in the end, what keeps the genus interesting. The velvet collectors and the bird's-nest growers are not really arguing about plants. They're arguing about what they want a plant to do in a room — disappear into a slow optical puzzle, or fill the space until the room becomes the plant.

Why the split matters for new collectors

If you are coming into anthuriums now, the temptation is to chase what is loudest on social media, which is almost always velvet. The photos travel better. The vocabulary is more romantic. The prices are more dramatic, which feels, perversely, like a guarantee of significance.

But a newer collector with a south-facing window and no humidifier will kill a crystallinum in a season and grow a magnificent A. plowmanii in the same spot without trying. The honest question is not which faction is better but which one matches the conditions you actually have, and the kind of attention you actually want to give. A velvet asks for vigilance. A bird's-nest asks for room.

Marcos and Devon have never met. They follow each other on one platform and ignore each other on two others. When I asked Marcos what he thought of jenmaniis, he said, after a long pause: "They're fine. They're just not the thing." When I asked Devon about dressleri, he laughed and said the same sentence back, almost word for word. Both men were, in their own way, completely right.

Common questions

What is the difference between velvet and bird's-nest anthuriums?
Velvet anthuriums are mostly section Cardiolonchium — small to medium epiphytes with matte, light-absorbing leaves and prominent silver venation, grown for surface and pattern. Bird's-nest anthuriums are section Pachyneurium — large terrestrials or lithophytes with leathery, paddle-shaped leaves arranged in a debris-catching rosette. The two groups have different care requirements, different aesthetics, and largely different collector bases.
Are bird's-nest anthuriums easier to grow than velvet anthuriums?
Generally yes, especially in average home conditions. Bird's-nests like A. plowmanii and A. jenmanii tolerate lower humidity, more direct light, and drier substrate than velvets, which usually demand 70%+ humidity, steady airflow, and a very airy bark-and-perlite mix to avoid root loss. The main constraint with bird's-nests is space rather than environment.
Is _Anthurium dressleri_ actually rare in cultivation?
True A. dressleri with reliable Panamanian provenance is uncommon and expensive, but plants sold under the name are widespread and often misidentified. Many circulating "dressleri" are hybrids or seed-grown plants of unclear lineage. Serious collectors buy from growers who can document the source clone, and learn to identify the species by petiole and venation rather than label.
Can you grow velvet and bird's-nest anthuriums in the same conditions?
With compromises, yes — a humid, bright room around 70–80°F suits both, but the substrate and watering will differ. Velvets want a chunky epiphytic mix kept lightly moist with high humidity, while bird's-nests prefer a heavier terrestrial mix that's allowed to dry between waterings. Most crossover growers keep them in the same room but on different shelves and schedules.
