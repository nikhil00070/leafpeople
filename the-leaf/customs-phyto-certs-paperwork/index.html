The Paper Trail

Every imported plant carries a folder thicker than its rootball — and the collectors who learn to read it stop losing shipments.

The box arrived in Miami on a Tuesday and sat on a concrete floor at the USDA inspection station until Friday. Inside were eleven Anthurium — a warocqueanum, two papillilaminum from a Panamanian nursery, a dressleri the seller swore was seed-grown, and a clutch of hybrids. By the time the broker called to say everything had cleared, four were mush. The grower in Panama had done his part. The phytosanitary certificate was clean. The CITES paperwork, where it applied, was in order. What had failed was the part nobody writes about: the four-day gap between paperwork passing and a human being opening the box.

Every collector who imports eventually learns that the documents are not the obstacle. The documents are the price of admission. The obstacle is the choreography around them — the broker who answers his phone, the inspector who works Thursdays, the grower who knows to write Philodendron sp. instead of a name that triggers a holdup. The paperwork no one explains is less about forms than about the unwritten rhythm of moving living tissue across a border that would prefer it didn't.

What the phyto actually is

A phytosanitary certificate is a one-page document issued by the exporting country's plant protection agency — Ecuador's Agrocalidad, Thailand's Department of Agriculture, Indonesia's Karantina Pertanian — declaring that a specific consignment has been inspected and found free of regulated pests and pathogens. It lists the botanical names, the quantity, the consignee, and any additional declarations the importing country requires. For the United States, that often means a line stating the plants were grown in an approved facility, or that they have been treated for a specific organism like Thrips palmi or root-knot nematodes.

The phyto is not a permit. It does not give you the right to import. It is a health affidavit from one government to another, and it expires — usually fourteen days from issuance, sometimes thirty. A phyto written on the first of the month for a shipment that leaves on the twentieth is worthless. Growers in remote provinces sometimes get this wrong, and there is no fixing it from the receiving end.

The certificate must match the box. Quantities matter. If the phyto says forty plants and the inspector counts forty-one, the entire consignment can be refused. If a species on the phyto isn't in the box, or a species in the box isn't on the phyto, the discrepancy is the inspector's call. Some are generous. Some are not. The grower's penmanship has, on more than one occasion, decided whether a Philodendron spiritus-sancti enters the country.

The documents are not the obstacle. They are the price of admission. The obstacle is the choreography no one writes down.

CITES, and the species that drag a second folder

The Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species is a separate regime layered on top. Most aroids in trade are not CITES-listed — your gloriosum, your melanochrysum, the standard verrucosum clones, none of these require CITES paperwork. But certain genera and species do, and the list is not intuitive. All Cycas are Appendix II. Many orchids. Some Nepenthes. Wild-collected specimens of certain species are restricted even when nursery-propagated ones are not.

For aroid collectors the practical bite comes from a handful of edges. Some Anthurium from specific protected habitats require export permits even if not CITES-listed, because the source country regulates them domestically. Wild-origin Philodendron from Colombia or Ecuador may be subject to national export bans regardless of international law. A grower offering a freshly collected Anthurium dressleri from the Darién is, at minimum, asking you to trust that he has the Panamanian collection and export permits in order. Usually he does not.

A CITES shipment requires both an export permit from the origin country and, for Appendix I species, an import permit from the destination — issued before the plant ships, not after. The permits must travel physically with the consignment. A scanned copy emailed ahead is not a substitute. Inspectors at JFK will not release a CITES box on the promise that the original is in the mail.

The small import permit that does most of the work

For US collectors the workhorse document is the USDA APHIS Small Lots of Seed permit's cousin: PPQ Form 587, the application for permit to import plants or plant products. Once granted, it produces a permit number and a set of green-and-yellow shipping labels that the foreign grower affixes to the outside of the box. Without that label, the shipment is routed to general inspection, which is slower, less careful, and held in conditions that no Anthurium enjoys.

The 587 takes weeks to issue the first time and is good for three years. It specifies which genera you may import, from which countries, and to which port of entry. A permit listing Miami does not let you receive at Los Angeles. A permit covering Philodendron and Anthurium does not cover the Begonia a friend talks you into adding to the order. New collectors routinely discover, halfway through their first import, that their permit is narrower than their wish list.

The permit also obliges you to a Plant Inspection Station. There are sixteen in the US, and the shipment must enter through one. Miami and Los Angeles handle the heaviest aroid traffic. Atlanta and New York are workable. Routing through anywhere else, in practice, means a hand-off and an extra day or two of bareroot plants in cardboard.

The week the plants don't get watered

This is the part the paperwork doesn't address. A shipment leaves Quito on Monday, arrives Miami Tuesday morning, clears the airline Tuesday afternoon, is delivered to the inspection station Wednesday, queued for inspection Thursday, inspected Friday, released to the broker late Friday, picked up Saturday, and forwarded by domestic courier Monday. That is a good week. A bad week adds a holiday, a backed-up station, or an inspector who flags one plant and holds the whole box for a lab culture.

Growers who ship well know this and pack for it. Sphagnum that holds moisture without staying wet enough to rot. Roots wrapped, not bare. Leaves trimmed back on species that won't miss them — a gloriosum ships better with two leaves than five. The box is labeled, vented, and not overstuffed. The bad shippers send you a beautiful plant in a bag of soaking peat and act surprised when it arrives black.

On the receiving end, the choreography matters as much. A good broker — and there are perhaps a dozen who specialize in ornamental plants at US ports — will pre-file the entry, walk the box through inspection, and have it on a domestic flight the same day it clears. A general freight broker will treat it like a pallet of car parts.

Why the rules exist, even when they cost you a plant

It is tempting, after losing a shipment, to treat the whole apparatus as bureaucratic theater. It is not. Thrips palmi established in a Florida greenhouse will spread to commercial agriculture within a season. Ralstonia solanacearum race 3 biovar 2 is a select agent — its arrival in a single ornamental shipment in 2003 cost the US nursery trade tens of millions of dollars in destroyed stock. The inspectors at the port are not hobbyist-friendly because they are not there for hobbyists. They are there for the tomato industry.

This is worth remembering when a box gets held for a thrip the grower swore wasn't there. The system is calibrated for the worst case, and the worst case has happened. Collectors who treat the inspection station as an adversary tend to have more problems than collectors who treat it as a slow, careful colleague.

It also clarifies why smuggling — the suitcase full of bareroot Anthurium that someone on a forum bragged about last year — is corrosive even when it doesn't get caught. Every undeclared plant that enters increases the political case for tightening the rules on people who do declare. The hobby's access to legal imports is more fragile than most collectors realize.

What to ask before you wire the money

Before paying a foreign grower, ask three questions and weigh the answers. Will you issue a phytosanitary certificate, and will you send a photograph of it before shipping? Will you write the species names exactly as they appear on my import permit? What date will you ship, and is that date within fourteen days of the phyto issuance? A grower who answers all three crisply has done this before. A grower who deflects on any of them is going to cost you a plant.

Ask also about the airway bill. A reputable grower books the shipment on a direct flight to your port of entry and sends the tracking number the moment it leaves the warehouse. Routing through a third country triples the handling and gives customs in the transit country an opportunity to open the box. Boxes opened in transit are rarely repacked well.

And keep a folder. Every import builds your file: which grower's papillilaminum arrived clean, which broker returns calls on Friday afternoons, which inspector at which station tends to flag what. The collectors who lose the fewest plants are not the ones with the best connections. They are the ones who write things down.
