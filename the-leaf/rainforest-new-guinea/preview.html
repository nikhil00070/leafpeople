Rainforest: New Guinea (Indonesia & PNG) — The Last Unmapped Forest

New Guinea's cloud-drowned mountains have produced the densest concentration of plant endemism on Earth — and botanists still haven't seen most of it.

Somewhere above 2,000 metres in the central cordillera of Papua New Guinea, the forest stops making sense by any lowland logic. The trees are shorter, gnarled, packed with moss so saturated it squeezes water with each step. Clouds sit in the canopy most of the day, and the light that reaches the understorey is dim, bluish, almost submarine. In that cold murk, a Hoya with leaves the texture of hammered pewter clings to a mossy trunk, its roots barely touching soil, pulling moisture from the air itself. Nobody has formally described it yet. It might not have a Latin name.

This is New Guinea in its ordinary state: extraordinary, incompletely known, still mid-invention. The island — shared between Indonesia's Papua provinces to the west and the independent nation of Papua New Guinea to the east — covers roughly 786,000 square kilometres and harbours somewhere north of 13,000 vascular plant species, of which an estimated 68 percent grow nowhere else on the planet. Those numbers are not stable. Taxonomists add new species from herbarium collections every year, and new collections arrive from valleys that field teams only recently reached for the first time.

Common questions

Why does New Guinea have so many plant species found nowhere else?
Tectonic collision between the Australian and Pacific Plates created the island's mountain spine relatively recently, producing thousands of isolated ridges and steep elevation gradients. Plant populations trapped in separate valleys diverged into new species over millions of years. The island also absorbed plant lineages from both Australia and tropical Asia, then elaborated them in isolation.
What Hoya species come from New Guinea?
New Guinea holds an estimated 150 to 200 Hoya species, making it the genus's greatest single concentration. Notable examples include Hoya archboldiana, with large crimson-centred flower clusters, and Hoya phyllura, with unusually narrow strap-like leaves. Many collected plants from the island remain formally undescribed and are traded under locality names rather than species epithets.
Are there aroids native to New Guinea?
Yes. The island has significant Rhaphidophora diversity — large climbing aroids unrelated to Monstera despite superficially similar leaf fenestration — as well as Pothos, Amorphophallus, and several undescribed Homalomena species. Mid-elevation cloud forests between roughly 1,500 and 2,500 metres are particularly underexplored and likely hold additional undescribed aroid species.
How do you care for Hoya species from New Guinea's highland forests?
Most highland New Guinea Hoya species prefer humidity above 70 percent, strong airflow, and a very fast-draining epiphytic substrate such as bark chunks or chunky sphagnum. They tolerate and often prefer cooler temperatures — roughly 16 to 22°C — and are stressed by sustained heat above 28°C. Bright indirect light or moderate LED intensity replicates the diffuse light of their cloud-forest habitat.
