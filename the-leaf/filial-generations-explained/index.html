F1, F2 and Selfing: What Hybrid Generation Labels Really Mean for What You're Buying

Three letters on a listing carry a surprising amount of weight — and a fair amount of fog.

The listing is short and the photo is good: Anthurium papillilaminum × dressleri, F2, $475, shipping Monday. You've been watching this seller for a year. You know the parent cross. You hover on the buy button and then stop on those two characters — F2 — and wonder, with the small private dread of someone who has been burned before, what they actually promise.

The aroid market runs on shorthand. F1, F2, BC, sib, selfed, IBE, RBE — abbreviations borrowed half from formal plant breeding and half from the hobbyist forums of the late 2000s, where growers traded seed and tried to sound rigorous. The labels matter. They also mislead. A serious buyer should know what each one means in textbook terms, what it tends to mean in practice on a Tuesday-night Instagram drop, and where the gap between those two definitions can cost you several hundred dollars.

What the letters mean on paper

In classical genetics, F1 — filial one — is the first generation of offspring from a cross between two distinct parents. If you pollinate a pure Philodendron gloriosum with pollen from a pure P. lynamii, every seed in that berry is, by definition, F1. F2 is what you get when you cross two F1 siblings to each other, or when you self an F1 — pollinate its spadix with its own pollen, assuming it produces fertile pollen and a receptive stigma in the same window, which in aroids it usually does not without intervention.

The reason breeders care about the distinction is Mendel. F1 offspring from two true parents tend to look uniform: the dominant traits show, recessive ones hide, and a tray of seedlings will largely resemble each other. F2 is where the deck reshuffles. Recessives surface. Variance explodes. You see runts and stars in the same flat.

So when a seller writes F1, they are claiming: these are first-generation hybrids from two named parents, and they should be reasonably consistent. When they write F2, they are claiming: these came from crossing two F1s, and they will not be consistent — some will be spectacular and some will be mediocre, and you are buying a ticket in that lottery.

An F1 label is a promise about parentage. An F2 label is a promise about variance — and variance is what you are actually buying.

What the letters mean in the greenhouse

The trouble is that the hobby's use of these terms drifted years ago. In a lot of listings, F1 has come to mean any cross between two species, even when the parents themselves were already hybrids or unverified clones. F2 has come to mean the second time someone bred this line, regardless of whether the second pairing involved siblings, a backcross, or a completely unrelated plant of the same nominal cross from another grower across the country.

This is not always dishonesty. Provenance in aroids is genuinely hard. A grower in Florida might receive pollen shipped from a collector in Panama whose mother plant was itself purchased from a Dutch nursery a decade ago. By the time a seedling reaches you, the lineage is a chain of trust, not a pedigree. Serious breeders — the ones whose names you can probably guess — keep records and will tell you exactly which clone of luxurians was crossed onto which clone of gloriosum, and when. Most sellers do not.

The practical consequence is that an F1 label tells you less than it should, and an F2 label tells you more than it pretends to. F2 is, in aroid practice, the honest admission that a plant came from a sibling cross or a self — and that the seller knows the offspring will vary.

Selfing, and why it changes the math

Selfing — fertilizing a plant with its own pollen — is common in anthuriums and increasingly in philodendrons, partly because it is the easiest way to produce more of a desirable individual without waiting for a second mature plant. Most aroids are protogynous: the stigma is receptive before the same inflorescence releases pollen, which is the plant's own quiet system against inbreeding. Breeders defeat this by storing pollen in the freezer, sometimes for months, and applying it to a later inflorescence on the same plant.

A selfed seedling is genetically an F2 in the loose sense — it carries a reshuffled version of its single parent's genome, with recessive traits suddenly visible. Selfing a hybrid mother (say, an exceptional papillilaminum × dressleri F1) produces a wide swing of seedlings: some will favor the papillilaminum side with its long, pendulous, bullate blades; some will favor the dressleri with its broader, blacker, more velvet leaves; some will look like neither parent and a few will look like both.

This is why selfed seedlings from a known exceptional individual can be both the best buy and the worst. The best, because you have a real chance at a plant that exceeds the parent. The worst, because for every superior seedling, several look like washed-out reversions, and the seller has often already pulled the obvious winners for their own collection.

Sib crosses, backcrosses, and the rest of the alphabet

Beyond F1 and F2, the vocabulary gets denser. A sib cross is two siblings from the same F1 batch bred to each other — genetically an F2, but sometimes labeled separately to indicate that two distinct individuals were used rather than a self. A backcross, often written BC1, is a hybrid bred back to one of its original parent species; growers use this to push a line closer to a particular look, usually because one parent contributed a trait — silver venation, narrow lobes, heavy texture — they want to deepen.

You will also see IBE (inbred) and line-bred in some anthurium circles, borrowed from orchid culture. These mean the breeder has run several generations of selfing or sibling crossing to stabilize a particular look, the way Anthurium warocqueanum 'Esqueleto' types have been pushed toward predictable narrow, long-bladed seedlings. Line-bred plants, in theory, breed true; in practice, true-breeding in this hobby is aspirational.

None of these labels are regulated. There is no governing body that says a seller may or may not call something F2. The label is descriptive at best, marketing at worst, and the only correction is buyer literacy.

What this means when you click buy

For most collectors, the practical rule is this: when you buy F1 from a careful breeder, you are buying a known cross — predictable, but rarely transcendent. When you buy F2 or selfed, you are buying variance, and variance only pays out if the parent stock was exceptional and the seller has not skimmed the cream.

Ask which clones were used. A good breeder will name them, sometimes with their own internal codes — 'my dark dressleri from 2019', 'the wide-form papillilaminum from Ree'. Ask whether the seller kept seedlings back. The answer is almost always yes; the follow-up question is whether the ones being sold are randomly selected or the rejects. Honest sellers will tell you. Some will even photograph the full flat.

Pay attention also to seedling age and selection point. An F2 sold as a two-leaf strap tells you nothing about the adult leaf. The same F2 sold at six leaves, after the seller has held them long enough to see venation and texture begin to express, is a much different purchase, and the price reflects it.

The honest case for buying F2

None of this is an argument against F2 or selfed seedlings. Some of the most interesting plants in private collections right now are F2s from crosses that no one would have predicted — the unrepeatable seedling out of a flat of twenty, the one that combined the velvet of one parent with the geometry of the other in a way the F1 generation never did. That seedling exists because someone took the gamble, and because variance, the thing that makes F2 risky, is also the thing that makes it generative.

The collectors who do best with F2 stock treat it the way a vineyard treats a vintage: they buy several, they grow them out, they wait. They accept that two of five will be ordinary and one might be extraordinary, and they sell or trade the middle of the pack to fund the next round. The ones who do worst buy one F2 seedling expecting it to look like the parent in the marketing photo, and feel cheated when it does not.

The label, in the end, is not a grade. It is a description of what kind of bet you are making. F1 is a small bet on consistency. F2 and selfed are larger bets on the tails of a distribution. Knowing which one you are placing — and asking the seller to confirm it in plain language — is most of what separates a collector from a customer.
