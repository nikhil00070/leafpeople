The Tells in the Tag

Every label is a small confession — about how long you've been at this, and how long you plan to stay.

The first thing I do in another collector's grow space, after the polite circuit of compliments, is look at the labels. Not the plants — the labels. A row of Anthurium seedlings under a humidifier can tell you only so much; the little plastic stakes wedged beside them tell you the rest. Whether the writing is in pencil or paint pen. Whether the Latin is spelled out or abbreviated to a private shorthand. Whether there is a cross, a date, a collector code, an Ecuagenera lot number, or nothing at all but a wishful magnificum in block capitals.

Collectors read each other this way without quite meaning to. The labels are a kind of dialect. You can tell, often within thirty seconds, whether someone is two years in or fifteen, whether they grew up in horticulture or arrived through Instagram, whether they intend to flower and cross their plants or simply keep them alive and beautiful on a shelf. It is one of the quietest status games in the hobby, and one of the most honest.

The Pencil-on-White-Plastic Orthodoxy

The oldest reliable system, and still the one used in most serious botanical gardens, is a soft graphite pencil — 2B is the usual choice — on a white polystyrene or vinyl stake. It looks like nothing. It is nearly invisible in a photograph. It is also, perversely, the most permanent option available to a home grower. Graphite does not fade in UV. It does not run when you mist. It survives a decade in a humid greenhouse with the legibility of the day it was written, which is more than can be said for almost any marker on the market.

When you see pencil-on-plastic in a private collection, you are usually looking at someone who has been burned. They tried Sharpies and watched the ink ghost off after eight months under T5s. They tried paint pens and discovered that the white ones flake and the black ones bleed. They tried a label printer and learned that thermal transfer ribbons crack in 80% humidity. Eventually they ended up where the Kew gardeners ended up a century ago, holding a carpenter's pencil over a bag of cheap stakes from a wholesale nursery.

There is a quiet snobbery in this. The pencil grower will not say anything about your gold metallic Sharpie, but they have noticed it, and they have made a small private judgment about how long your Philodendron spiritus-sancti cutting is going to remain identifiable as such.

A label is a promise to your future self that you will still care, in three years, what this plant actually is.

The Paint Pen Generation

Paint pens — usually a Posca PC-3M or PC-5M, white ink on a black plastic stake — are the dominant look of the post-2020 collector. They photograph beautifully. They read across a room. They have a slight handmade warmth, like chalk on slate. For about eighteen months they are perfect, and then, depending on the conditions, they begin to chalk, smudge, or lift off in sheets when wet.

This is the label of someone whose collection is, at least partly, a visual project. There is nothing wrong with that. A grow shelf is a thing you live with; it should look like something. But the paint pen tells you the owner is thinking about the next photograph as well as the next repot. You will often find paint-pen labels in collections where the substrate is also styled — orchid bark on top of a layer of sphagnum, a single piece of charcoal placed visibly at the rim.

The honest paint-pen growers rewrite their labels every spring. The dishonest ones let them fade to a ghost of Anthurium warocqueanum and quietly hope nobody asks which clone.

Engraved Aluminum and the Long Game

Then there is the collector who has gone to engraved aluminum. These are the soft metal tags, usually about three inches long, that you scratch into with a ballpoint pen — the pressure embosses the surface and the writing is, for practical purposes, eternal. They were standard issue in serious rare-fruit and palm collections for most of the twentieth century. They are now appearing, in small numbers, in aroid rooms.

An aluminum tag is a declaration. It says: this plant is going to outlive my current apartment. It says I have thought about what happens to this Philodendron when I move, when I divide it, when I ship a cutting to a friend in Singapore and want them to know exactly which mother plant it came from. The information density is usually higher, too — a full binomial, a clone designation, a date of acquisition, sometimes the source. P. gloriosum 'dark form' / ex. Equagenera / 03-2021.

You almost never see aluminum tags on a one-year-old collection. They are the mark of someone who has lost plants and learned from it, or who came in from another collecting discipline — orchids, cycads, citrus — where multi-decade record-keeping is simply how things are done.

The Handwriting Itself

Beyond the material, there is the hand. Collectors who came up through botany or horticulture tend to write the genus in full and capitalize correctly: Anthurium, not anthurium, not ANTHURIUM. They use the multiplication sign for hybrids — Philodendron × evansii — rather than a lowercase x. They put cultivar names in single quotes and never italicize them. None of this is affectation; it is the convention they learned in a lab or a herbarium, and it sits in the hand like a regional accent.

Self-taught collectors develop their own conventions, which are often more practical and more idiosyncratic. I have seen labels that read PHIL VERR DF for Philodendron verrucosum dark form, the abbreviations as private as a diary. I have seen seedling trays marked only with a four-digit cross code, the parentage kept in a spreadsheet at the grower's day job. I have seen one woman's collection where every label is in her grandmother's pre-war German cursive, because that is the handwriting she grew up watching.

You can fake the look of expertise on Instagram. You cannot fake a label drawer that has been maintained, consistently, for eight years.

What the Date Tells Us

A date on a label is one of the most revealing details in the whole hobby. No date at all suggests a collector who is living plant-to-plant — buying, killing, replacing, not tracking. A purchase date suggests someone who wants to know how long a plant has been in their care. A repot date suggests someone who has had root issues and is now disciplined about it. A flowering date suggests a hybridizer, or someone hoping to become one.

The most serious collectors I know carry two dates: acquisition and last repot. Some add a third — last fertilizer change, or the date a plant was moved from semi-hydro back into bark. These people are not eccentric. They are doing what every botanical garden does, on a smaller scale, because they have learned that memory is unreliable and the plant does not care about your good intentions.

If you want to look like you know what you are doing, write the date. If you want to actually know what you are doing, write the date and then look at it next year.

The Collector Code

At the deep end of the pool, you find collectors who have given up on labels as identification and started using them as index keys. The label says only AR-0147. The actual information — species, source, parent plant, propagation method, notes on growth — lives in a notebook or a database. This is how serious Anthurium breeders in Florida and the Netherlands have worked for decades, and how the better aroid hybridizers in Southeast Asia work now.

The system has obvious advantages. You can rename a plant when taxonomy shifts, as it constantly does in Philodendron and Anthurium, without scraping any tags. You can record sensitive information — who gave you the cutting, what you paid — without broadcasting it to every visitor. You can move a plant between rooms and the record follows.

It also signals, unmistakably, a collector who plans to be doing this in ten years. Nobody sets up an accession-numbering system for a hobby they are going to abandon.

What Mine Say, and What Yours Probably Should

My own labels are a compromise and probably a confession. Pencil on white plastic for the bulk of the collection, because I learned the hard way. Engraved aluminum on the plants I consider permanent — the gloriosum that came from a friend who has since left the hobby, the luxurians that flowered last spring, the dressleri I am not ready to talk about. Paint pen on the shelf I photograph, because I am not above it.

If you are starting out, the practical advice is dull and correct: buy a box of plain white stakes and a 2B pencil and write the full binomial, the source, and the date. Rewrite anything that fades. Keep a notebook, even a bad one. The label is not for the plant. The plant knows what it is. The label is for you, three years from now, when the plant has tripled in size and you cannot quite remember whether it came from a reputable nursery in Quito or a stranger on a forum at two in the morning.

And when another collector wanders past your shelf and pauses, just slightly, over a tag — they are not reading the Latin. They already know the Latin. They are reading you.

Rare plants, real stories — a few times a week.
