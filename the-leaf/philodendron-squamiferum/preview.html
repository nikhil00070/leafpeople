Touch the Stem

Philodendron squamiferum

Fuzzy red-bristled petioles, D-shaped cross-sections, and the quiet argument that half an aroid's beauty lives below the leaf blade.

Run your thumb along the petiole of Philodendron squamiferum and it catches — not unpleasantly, like dragging a fingertip across velvet the wrong way. The scales are stiff, faintly iridescent, deep red against pale green. The leaf at the top is fine: deeply lobed, oak-leaf silhouette, good size. But the leaf is almost beside the point. People who keep squamiferum know that you grow it for the stem.

This is the argument: collectors have organized almost all of their desire around leaf blades. We talk about bullation, silver tessellation, the precise ratio of primary vein to interveinal tissue, whether a velvet surface reads green or near-black under different light. We are not wrong to care about those things. But the petiole — and by extension the cataphyll, the internode, the stem surface — is where a plant's character often concentrates. It is also where taxonomy shudders, where provenance leaks through, and where the most tactile pleasures of the hobby live. We should pay closer attention.
