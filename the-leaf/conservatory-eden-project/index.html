Botanical Tour: The Eden Project Biomes, Cornwall, UK — The Largest Rainforest Under a Roof

Inside a worked-out china-clay crater in Cornwall, the Eden Project engineered a self-sustaining tropical world — and the plants climbing its trees are the same ones collectors obsess over at home.

On the road into St Austell you wouldn't guess it was there. Cornwall gives you hedgerows, granite outcrops, the particular grey-green of Atlantic sky. Then the road bends and the land simply drops away, and at the bottom of a vast white-walled pit — a crater left by a century of kaolinite extraction — two clusters of geodesic domes sit in the earth like something that landed there. The scale disorients you before you even reach the gate. Once you're inside and descending the terraced paths toward the biomes, the domes grow until they block out the horizon, their hexagonal cushions catching the light like the compound eye of some enormous insect. The effect is not of a greenhouse. It is of architecture with ambition proportional to the landscape around it.

The Eden Project opened in 2001. The idea came from Tim Smit — who had earlier helped restore the Lost Gardens of Heligan, also in Cornwall — and the biomes were designed by architect Nicholas Grimshaw. The structural logic was straightforward in conception and staggering in execution: rather than glass, which would have been prohibitively heavy for the irregular geometry involved, the domes are clad in ETFE foil cushions. ETFE — ethylene tetrafluoroethylene — is a transparent polymer that weighs a fraction of glass, admits ultraviolet light effectively, and can be inflated to form a rigid, self-cleaning panel. The hexagonal cushions vary in size as the dome curves, but together they create an envelope that traps heat and humidity while flooding the interior with diffuse natural light. It is the kind of engineering that looks inevitable only after someone has already done it.

What the Pit Became

China clay — kaolinite — was extracted from this corner of Cornwall for roughly two centuries to supply the ceramics and paper industries. The pits left behind were deep, white-walled, and considered by most people to be useless. When the Eden Project's founders looked at the St Austell pit, they saw a pre-built shelter from prevailing winds, a sunken microclimate, and several hectares of vertical wall that could anchor terracing. What read as ruin was, in agricultural and horticultural terms, a kind of opportunity. The pit's depth means the biomes sit below the surrounding land, partially insulated from the cold Atlantic weather above.

The excavated floor was too hostile to sustain plants immediately — the pit had no topsoil whatsoever. Before a single seed went in, the project imported and manufactured an enormous quantity of soil-like substrate, mixing composted material with local waste to create something plants could root into. This effort, often underreported in accounts of the place, is in some ways the real founding act of Eden: not the architecture but the soil-making, the decision that a dead industrial site could be persuaded to sustain life if you were willing to begin at the very beginning.

The Rainforest Biome is the larger of the two main structures. The Mediterranean Biome sits adjacent and houses a different climate zone — drought-tolerant plants from South Africa, California, and the Mediterranean basin — but it is the rainforest structure that draws the botanically serious visitor. By the measure of interior volume and plant diversity, it remains the largest indoor rainforest on Earth.

A whole tropical forest, self-sustaining and humid, rising out of a china-clay crater in the far southwest of England — that is still a genuinely strange thing to stand inside.

Engineering Humidity

Running a self-contained tropical environment in southwest England requires constant mechanical intervention, and the Eden team has made that infrastructure part of the experience rather than hiding it. Misting systems installed throughout the Rainforest Biome produce the kind of ground-level and canopy-level fog that keeps humidity in the range tropical plants require. A waterfall — large enough to hear before you see it — adds both moisture and the sound signature that most visitors associate with real jungle: constant water in motion.

Heat management in a space this large is a serious engineering problem. The ETFE cushions admit solar gain, which helps, but the Cornwall climate cannot be relied upon for warmth year-round. Supplemental heating runs through the biome to maintain temperatures that the plants — recruited from tropical South America, West Africa, Malaysia, and Oceania — would recognise. The result is that stepping from the outdoor Cornish air into the biome involves a physical transition: the air thickens, the temperature rises, and the smell changes to something green and faintly fungal, the smell of decomposition and growth happening simultaneously.

What's engineered here is not a simulation of a rainforest in any theatrical sense. The plants are real, the humidity is measured, the soil is managed. The biome is working, in the horticultural sense — the trees are growing, the canopy is developing, the understorey is filling in. Staff manage the space the way a botanical garden manages any living collection, with regular assessment, pruning, intervention where needed, and the kind of patient attention that large-scale tropical horticulture demands.

The Canopy and the Cloud Bridge

The Rainforest Biome is tall enough to have a functioning canopy — mature trees that have grown into the upper reaches of the structure — and the designers capitalised on this with a walkway and a rope bridge at height. From the canopy walkway you look down into the planting rather than across it, which reverses the usual visitor experience of a botanical garden. From below, a tree canopy reads as a ceiling. From above, it reads as a surface: a continuous, irregular plane of green with gaps where the light comes through.

The cloud bridge connects two elevated points within the biome and gives a view that is genuinely disorienting in the right way — you are inside a dome, above trees, in Cornwall. The ETFE panels are visible from here at close range, and the scale of the individual cushions becomes legible. On overcast days, diffuse light fills the space evenly; on bright days, the hexagonal geometry casts soft shadows across the upper canopy. Neither conditions seems like the one the space was designed for, which is perhaps the point: the structure works in any light Cornwall offers.

The lookout platform at the top of the biome's internal path provides the widest panoramic view of the whole planted interior. From here, the planting reads as a genuine forest — not as rows of specimens but as a layered ecosystem with distinct storeys. This is worth pausing over, because it is not a given. Many large conservatories feel like collections. The Rainforest Biome feels, by the time the trees have grown into each other, like a place.

The Plants That Collectors Recognise

The biome's headline plants are economic species — bananas (Musa spp.), cacao (Theobroma cacao), rubber (Hevea brasiliensis) — planted partly to explain commodity histories to a general audience. These are interesting in their own right and worth finding. But the plants that will stop a collector mid-path are the ones climbing the trees and colonising the understorey: aroids, epiphytes, ferns, and the species that in a different context live in a humidity tent or a dedicated grow room.

Philodendron species climb the biome's supporting structures and tree trunks the way they climb in habitat — using aerial roots, moving upward toward light, producing increasingly large and differentiated leaves as the plant matures. Mature specimens in a biome of this scale express leaf forms that most growers never see domestically, because the conditions that trigger full adult morphology — consistent humidity, warm temperatures, the mechanical stimulus of a real vertical surface to climb — are difficult to sustain in a home environment. The same is true of the anthuriums: grown in the ground or in deep substrate at the base of trees, given years rather than seasons, Anthurium species reach a scale and a leaf texture that a collector growing in a 6-inch pot simply does not encounter.

The epiphyte layer — bromeliads, orchids, and smaller aroids perched in the forks of trees and on horizontal branches — repays close attention. The spatial organisation mirrors what happens in a real forest: different species have colonised different heights and aspects, responding to light levels and moisture gradients. For a collector who spends time thinking about where in the canopy a given species originates, this is useful to see rendered at scale. It clarifies why a Hoya from a mid-elevation cloud forest behaves differently in cultivation than one collected near sea level, or why a terrestrial Anthurium needs a different substrate than one whose roots never touch ground.

What's in the Neighbouring Biome

The Mediterranean Biome is smaller and drier, and its plant palette — Olea trees, cork oak, proteas, California poppies — is largely non-overlapping with the rainforest collection. For the collector primarily interested in aroids and tropical epiphytes it is secondary, but it rewards a walk. The contrast in air quality between the two biomes is immediately apparent: you move from humid, warm, and green-smelling air into something that feels closer to a warm afternoon on a hillside — drier, slightly dusty, herby. The architectural experience of the domes is also worth examining from the inside of both: the Mediterranean Biome has different proportions and the planting sits lower, so the structure reads differently.

There is outdoor planting across the site as well — the terraces that descend into the pit are planted with species suited to the Cornish climate — but for the plants a serious grower cares about, the two biomes are the destination. Allow more time than you think you need in the Rainforest Biome specifically. The understorey reveals itself slowly, and the plants worth finding are not always at eye level.

Why a Collector Should Go

The practical value of visiting the Eden Project, for someone who grows tropical plants at home, is the experience of scale and of context. A Philodendron gloriosum in a 10-inch nursery pot, grown on a shelf under a grow light, is a different object from the same species seen climbing an actual tree in conditions close to what it evolved for. The biome doesn't show you how to grow plants in a domestic environment, but it does show you what you are attempting to approximate and why certain interventions — the humidity, the airflow, the climbing support, the substrate depth — matter in the ways they do.

There is also something less practical and harder to articulate: the experience of standing inside a humid rainforest inside a geodesic dome inside a china-clay pit in Cornwall is genuinely strange, and the strangeness doesn't wear off quickly. A whole tropical forest, self-sustaining and humid, rising out of an industrial crater in the far southwest of England — that is still a genuinely strange thing to stand inside. The absurdity of it, which is also its ambition, is part of why the place has resonated with people who have no particular interest in plants. For people who do have that interest, it is the scale and the specificity of what's growing that holds attention long after the architecture has been admired.

If you go, go slowly. Take the canopy walkway early before crowds fill it. Find the base of the waterfall and stand there. Look for the aroids climbing out of sight into the upper canopy and try to identify what you're seeing. Some of what's up there you've probably grown in a considerably smaller pot at home.

Getting There and Planning a Visit

The Eden Project is located near St Austell in Cornwall, accessible by train to St Austell station with connections onward, or by road. It is a substantial site and most serious visitors spend a full day. The crowds are real — Eden draws large numbers, particularly in summer — so arriving early in the day improves the biome experience considerably. Weekdays out of the main summer season are quieter.

The site is open most of the year but has seasonal hours, so checking current opening times before travelling is essential. Eden has expanded over the years with outdoor events and installations, particularly in summer and around winter holidays, which affect the character of the visit significantly. If plants are the primary reason you're going, a midweek visit in late spring or early autumn puts you in the biomes with fewer people and in conditions where the outdoor planting is also worth attention.

Common questions

What is the Eden Project and why is it famous?
The Eden Project is a large visitor attraction near St Austell in Cornwall, built inside a former china-clay extraction pit. It is best known for its two enormous geodesic biomes clad in ETFE foil cushions — one housing the largest indoor rainforest in the world, the other a Mediterranean climate environment. It opened in 2001 and was conceived by Tim Smit, with architecture by Nicholas Grimshaw.
What plants can you see in the Rainforest Biome?
The Rainforest Biome contains tropical plants from South America, West Africa, Malaysia, and Oceania, including bananas, cacao, rubber trees, philodendrons, anthuriums, bromeliads, orchids, and other epiphytes. Many specimens have been growing for years and reach sizes rarely seen outside their native habitat. The biome is tall enough to have a functioning multi-storey canopy.
What should I not miss at the Eden Project?
The canopy walkway and rope bridge in the Rainforest Biome offer views down into the tree canopy that are unlike anything at ground level. The cloud bridge and the lookout platform also provide distinctive vantage points. The waterfall inside the rainforest biome is worth finding, and the Mediterranean Biome next door provides a striking contrast in both plant palette and atmosphere.
When is the best time to visit the Eden Project?
The site is open most of the year, but hours vary seasonally. A midweek visit in late spring or early autumn tends to offer smaller crowds and good conditions for seeing both the biomes and the outdoor planting. Summer brings the largest visitor numbers. Always check the Eden Project's official site for current opening times before travelling.
