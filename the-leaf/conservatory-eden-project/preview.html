Botanical Tour: The Eden Project, Cornwall, UK — The Largest Rainforest Under a Roof

Inside a worked-out china-clay crater in Cornwall, the Eden Project engineered a self-sustaining tropical world — and the plants climbing its trees are the same ones collectors obsess over at home.

On the road into St Austell you wouldn't guess it was there. Cornwall gives you hedgerows, granite outcrops, the particular grey-green of Atlantic sky. Then the road bends and the land simply drops away, and at the bottom of a vast white-walled pit — a crater left by a century of kaolinite extraction — two clusters of geodesic domes sit in the earth like something that landed there. The scale disorients you before you even reach the gate. Once you're inside and descending the terraced paths toward the biomes, the domes grow until they block out the horizon, their hexagonal cushions catching the light like the compound eye of some enormous insect. The effect is not of a greenhouse. It is of architecture with ambition proportional to the landscape around it.

The Eden Project opened in 2001. The idea came from Tim Smit — who had earlier helped restore the Lost Gardens of Heligan, also in Cornwall — and the biomes were designed by architect Nicholas Grimshaw. The structural logic was straightforward in conception and staggering in execution: rather than glass, which would have been prohibitively heavy for the irregular geometry involved, the domes are clad in ETFE foil cushions. ETFE — ethylene tetrafluoroethylene — is a transparent polymer that weighs a fraction of glass, admits ultraviolet light effectively, and can be inflated to form a rigid, self-cleaning panel. The hexagonal cushions vary in size as the dome curves, but together they create an envelope that traps heat and humidity while flooding the interior with diffuse natural light. It is the kind of engineering that looks inevitable only after someone has already done it.

Common questions

What is the Eden Project and why is it famous?
The Eden Project is a large visitor attraction near St Austell in Cornwall, built inside a former china-clay extraction pit. It is best known for its two enormous geodesic biomes clad in ETFE foil cushions — one housing the largest indoor rainforest in the world, the other a Mediterranean climate environment. It opened in 2001 and was conceived by Tim Smit, with architecture by Nicholas Grimshaw.
What plants can you see in the Rainforest Biome?
The Rainforest Biome contains tropical plants from South America, West Africa, Malaysia, and Oceania, including bananas, cacao, rubber trees, philodendrons, anthuriums, bromeliads, orchids, and other epiphytes. Many specimens have been growing for years and reach sizes rarely seen outside their native habitat. The biome is tall enough to have a functioning multi-storey canopy.
What should I not miss at the Eden Project?
The canopy walkway and rope bridge in the Rainforest Biome offer views down into the tree canopy that are unlike anything at ground level. The cloud bridge and the lookout platform also provide distinctive vantage points. The waterfall inside the rainforest biome is worth finding, and the Mediterranean Biome next door provides a striking contrast in both plant palette and atmosphere.
When is the best time to visit the Eden Project?
The site is open most of the year, but hours vary seasonally. A midweek visit in late spring or early autumn tends to offer smaller crowds and good conditions for seeing both the biomes and the outdoor planting. Summer brings the largest visitor numbers. Always check the Eden Project's official site for current opening times before travelling.
