The Four Substrate Recipes I Keep Coming Back To

A decade of mixing mediums in a spare-bedroom jungle, distilled to four blends that actually pull their weight.

My kitchen scale is older than my oldest Philodendron gloriosum, and it lives on the same shelf as the bark. That should tell you something. The shelf above it holds five-gallon buckets of pumice, orchid bark in three grades, coco chips that have been rinsed within an inch of their lives, sphagnum from two suppliers because I trust neither completely, perlite, charcoal, worm castings in a yogurt tub, and a brick of coir I keep meaning to throw out. The scale is for the recipes I actually use.

I used to mix by feel. Most of us start that way, scooping handfuls of this and that until the pot looks right, and for the easy plants it works. Then you kill a verrucosum in a peat-heavy big-box mix, or watch the roots on an Anthurium warocqueanum turn to mush in something that drained beautifully in your living room but compacted in the basement under lights, and you start writing things down. What follows are the four recipes I keep coming back to. Not because they're clever. Because over years and many plants, they fail the least.

Why four, and not one

The single-mix evangelists are loud online, and I understand the appeal. One bucket, one scoop, every plant gets the same thing. It simplifies repotting day. It simplifies your shelving. If you grow mostly chunky-rooted climbing philodendrons in a humid greenhouse, one aggressive bark mix probably is enough.

But I don't grow one thing. A thin-rooted Anthurium luxurians wants a different physical environment than a corm-forming Amydrium medium or a rhizomatous begonia trailing across a shallow pan. The roots are shaped differently, they breathe differently, they recover from drought differently. Pretending otherwise is how you end up with one happy plant and three sulking ones.

The four mixes below correspond to four root behaviors I see again and again: climbing aroids with thick aerial roots; thin-rooted epiphytic anthuriums and hoyas; terrestrial crawlers like gloriosum and mamei that want a flat, moist-but-airy bed; and rhizomatous begonias, which are their own peculiar world. If you grow across these categories, you need at least this many recipes. Probably you need a fifth for seedlings, but that's another essay.

Substrate is not a recipe so much as a hypothesis about how water will move through a pot in your house, under your light, in your air.

Mix one: the climbing aroid chunk

By volume: four parts medium orchid bark (fir or pine, 1/4 to 1/2 inch), two parts pumice, one part perlite, one part horticultural charcoal, and a handful — maybe a cup per five-gallon batch — of long-fiber sphagnum torn into thumbnail pieces. No coco, no peat, no soil. This is what goes around the totem for Philodendron verrucosum, melanochrysum, gigas, Monstera dubia, the big climbing Epipremnum, and most of the velvet-leaved Philodendrons sold as 'climbers' even when they're young enough to be in a 4-inch pot.

The logic is simple. Thick aerial roots want to grip and breathe. Bark gives them structure. Pumice holds water inside its pores without staying wet on the surface. The sphagnum, scattered through rather than layered, gives the fine feeder roots something to latch onto in the gaps. Charcoal is half ritual, half hedge against the inevitable moment something sits too wet for a week.

I water this mix until it pours out the bottom, then again two minutes later, and then I leave it alone until the top two inches feel dry to a finger pushed in deep. In my apartment, with 65% humidity and decent airflow, that's about every five to seven days in summer, every ten in winter. If you grow drier, swap one part perlite for one more part sphagnum and check sooner.

Mix two: the thin-root epiphyte blend

This is the one I rebuild most often, because it's for the plants I love most and worry about most: Anthurium warocqueanum, regale, papillilaminum, dressleri when I'm lucky enough to have one, plus the harder hoyas — clemensiorum, imperialis, the thick-leaved meredithii types. The recipe: three parts fine orchid bark (1/8 to 1/4 inch), three parts pumice or small lava, two parts perlite, one part charcoal, one part sphagnum torn small. Some growers will recognize this as a close cousin of the so-called 'Aroid Mix' that circulates in Florida greenhouses, and that's because I learned it, more or less, from a grower in Apopka who shrugged when I asked for proportions and said 'looks about right when you stir it.'

The whole mix should look almost mineral when you hold a handful. Light, sharp-edged, dry-feeling even when damp. Thin-rooted anthuriums rot at the slightest provocation in anything denser, but they also resent being bone-dry — the fine roots are the first to crisp. You're aiming for a substrate that drains in two seconds and then holds a film of moisture against every particle for a day or two.

I run this in net pots inside cache pots for the queens, which lets me lift and check root condition without disturbing anything. If the roots look chalk-white and firm, I leave them alone for another month. If I see browning at the tips, I'm watering too often, or the basement got cold, or both.

Mix three: the terrestrial crawler bed

Philodendron gloriosum, mamei, plowmanii, pastazanum — the crawlers — get a different treatment, and this is where most new collectors go wrong. They reach for the climbing aroid chunk mix because it's what the internet shouts about, and then they wonder why the rhizome on their gloriosum keeps stalling. Crawlers grow horizontally along a substrate surface; their roots fan downward in shallow plumes. They want something flatter, finer, and slightly more moisture-retentive than the climbers.

The mix: three parts fine bark, two parts pumice, two parts coco chips (well-rinsed, the chunky kind, not coir), one part perlite, one part worm castings, one part sphagnum. The coco and castings are the difference. They hold a steadier moisture across a shallow pot without going anaerobic, and the castings give a slow trickle of nutrition that crawlers seem to appreciate. I pot these in long, low rectangular planters — restaurant supply stores sell wonderful cheap ones — so the rhizome has room to march.

Water when the top inch is dry, never let the rhizome itself sit in wet substrate, and bury only the roots, not the rhizome. People bury the rhizome and then write me sad emails three months later. Don't bury the rhizome.

Mix four: the begonia pan mix

Rhizomatous and rex begonias are the outliers in any aroid-heavy collection, and they punish you for treating them like aroids. They want more organic matter, finer particle size, and a substrate that's genuinely moisture-retentive without being soggy. The mix I land on, again and again: two parts fine bark, two parts coco chips, two parts perlite, one part pumice, one part worm castings, one part sphagnum, and — this is the part that horrifies the chunky-mix purists — one part actual peat-based potting soil, the cheap stuff, sieved to remove the largest chunks.

I know. I know. But begonias like a little body in their substrate, and the peat-based fraction gives just enough moisture buffering that I can leave them three or four days between waterings without crisping the leaf margins. Begonia amphioxus, the bipinnatifid types, U-413, the listada-line cultivars — all of them sit happily in this mix in 4 to 6-inch shallow pots.

If you grow begonias under lights in dry air, increase the sphagnum and skip the soil. If you grow them in a humid case, decrease the sphagnum. The recipe is a starting point, not a contract.

What changes between houses

Every substrate recipe is a hypothesis about your conditions, and the conditions are doing more of the work than the recipe is. I moved from a top-floor apartment with iron radiators and dry winter air to a brick rowhouse with a damp basement, and every one of these mixes had to be re-tuned. Same proportions, different watering frequency, different pot choice, different outcomes.

The variables that actually move the needle: ambient humidity, airflow over the pot surface, pot material (terracotta dries twice as fast as plastic), pot size relative to root mass, and the temperature where the pots actually sit, which is often five degrees off the room reading. If you change one of those, the recipe behaves differently. This is why copying a famous grower's mix and getting bad results doesn't mean the mix is wrong. It means you're not in their grow room.

So treat the four recipes above as a baseline. Mix a small batch. Pot a single plant. Watch it for a season. If the substrate is dry in two days, add more sphagnum or coco. If it's still wet in two weeks, add pumice and perlite. Write down what you changed. The notebook is the recipe; the bucket is just where it lives.

A note on the things I no longer use

Leca had a long run in my collection and I've mostly retired it, except for propagation. The plants grew, but they grew differently — thinner leaves, longer internodes, a slightly washed-out quality I couldn't shake even with proper hydroponic nutrients. Some growers get gorgeous results in semi-hydro. I'm not one of them, and I've stopped pretending.

Pure sphagnum, the other internet darling, is brilliant for rooting cuttings and disastrous for established plants in any pot larger than 4 inches. It compacts, goes anaerobic at the core, and hides rot until the plant tips over. I use it in the mixes, never as the mix.

And peat: I keep one bag for the begonia pan recipe and grudgingly use it. Coir doesn't quite replace it for that one application, in my experience, though I keep testing. If you've found a substitute that holds the same moisture profile without going hydrophobic when it dries, write to me. I'll change the recipe. That's what the notebook is for.
