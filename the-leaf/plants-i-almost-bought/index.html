The Plants I Almost Bought

A confession about the rare aroids I came within minutes of wiring money for, and the quiet voice that stopped me each time.

The cart is full at 11:47 on a Tuesday. The grower is in Chiang Mai, the listing is a mother plant of Anthurium papillilaminum with a leaf the color of wet asphalt, and the price, converted, is somewhere between a used motorcycle and a small surgery. My banking app is open in another tab. The wire instructions are pasted into a notes file. My thumb hovers.

I have, over six years of collecting, hovered like this maybe a dozen times. Sometimes I press send. More often — and this is the part nobody writes about — I close the tab. Not because I came to my senses in any noble way, but because something specific, small, and usually pedantic interrupted the hypnosis. A petiole cross-section in the third photo. A grower's reply that took eleven days. A leaf shape I'd seen before, on a much cheaper plant, under a different name. What follows is a catalog of those interruptions.

The Ecuadorian dressleri that wasn't

The first near-miss I remember clearly involved a seller in Guayaquil and a plant listed as Anthurium dressleri, the true Panamanian black-leaf form. The photos were extraordinary: bullate, deep oxblood new growth hardening to a near-black mature leaf, the kind of velvet that swallows a phone flash. The asking price was four figures, which at the time felt aspirational rather than insane.

What stopped me was the back of the leaf. Dressleri has a particular geography to it — a sinus that closes tightly, lobes that meet or nearly overlap, a midrib that runs pale against the dark lamina. The plant in the photo had open lobes and a green underside that read more like a hybrid of papillilaminum and something else. I asked the seller for a clear shot of the petiole and the back of a mature leaf. He sent a different plant entirely. We are not, I told him politely, going to do business.

Months later I saw the same photos surface under a different account, with the species name changed to Anthurium sp. "Colombia." Whoever finally bought it got a perfectly nice anthurium. They did not get a dressleri.

The expensive mistake is rarely the plant. It's the story you told yourself about the plant at one in the morning.

The variegated spiritus-sancti

There is a market in things that shouldn't exist, and a variegated Philodendron spiritus-sancti is close to the top of it. The species itself is endangered in the wild, restricted to a few populations in Espírito Santo, and even non-variegated tissue-cultured plants run into the thousands. A stable variegate would be the kind of plant that justifies its own insurance rider.

The listing I almost bought was from a private collector in Java, a single rooted cutting, half-moon variegation, photographed against a black cloth in the way one photographs jewelry. The price was, frankly, the price of a car. I had the money. I had, briefly, the will.

What stopped me was a video. He sent it on request — the plant turning under a grow light, the variegation moving across the lamina. The white sectors were too crisp, the line between green and cream too geometric. Stable variegation in spiritus-sancti, where it exists, tends to feather and bleed. This looked painted. I do not know if it was painted. I know I asked for a second video taken in daylight and never received one.

The Philodendron "Patriciae" with the wrong drip tip

Philodendron patriciae is a long, pleated, strap-like plant from the Chocó, and the thing that makes it patriciae rather than one of its many lookalikes — campii, certain forms of verrucosum × something — is a combination of corrugation depth, a particular bronze cast on emergence, and a long, narrow drip tip that extends well past the rest of the leaf in a way that looks almost theatrical.

The plant I almost bought had everything but the drip tip. The leaves were narrow, the pleating was there, the color on the new growth was correct. But the apex was blunt — rounded, even — and that is not what patriciae does. Patriciae tapers like a calligraphy stroke.

I sent the photos to a friend who has grown the species for a decade. She replied with one word: "campii." The asking price was about three times what a campii should cost. I bought a confirmed campii from a US grower two weeks later for a fifth of the original quote and have never been sorry.

The hoya with the suspicious peduncle

Not every near-miss is four figures. One of the ones I think about most was a forty-dollar cutting of Hoya sp. "Sabah Black," the dark-flowered form people chase because the corollas come out so deeply maroon they read as actual black under most light.

The cutting was fine. The seller was fine. What stopped me was that the photograph of the bloom — the one anchoring the whole listing — showed a peduncle attached to a leaf node that didn't match the leaves on the cutting being sold. Hoyas bloom from persistent peduncles; a peduncle is, in a real sense, a fingerprint. The bloom photo and the cutting photo had been taken from two different plants.

Maybe both plants were the same clone. Maybe not. The seller couldn't tell me, which itself was the answer. I have learned to treat "I'm not sure, I got it from someone else" as a complete sentence ending the transaction.

The Anthurium forgetii with no white veins

Forgetii is the peltate one — the leaf attaches in the middle, no sinus, a perfect shield. The classic clone, the one most collectors actually want, has bright silver-white primary veins on a matte dark green ground. There's also a green-veined form, which is botanically interesting and aesthetically much less interesting, and which costs roughly a tenth as much.

The plant I almost bought was photographed in a way that hid the venation. Wet leaves, hard flash, a strategic angle. The price was for the silver-veined clone. The listing text said "silver veined." The photos did not actually show silver veins; they showed reflections that could have been silver veins or could have been water.

I asked for a dry photo in indirect light. The seller delivered, to his credit, and the veins were green. He adjusted the price. I declined anyway, because by then I had stopped wanting the plant and started wanting not to be the kind of person who has to negotiate a seller down from a misleading listing.

The import that would have arrived in February

One of the cleanest passes I ever made was on a plant I genuinely wanted: a mature Philodendron luxurians, the Chocó species with the broad, quilted, almost rubbery dark leaves and the petioles that feel like suede. The grower was reputable. The price was reasonable. The phytosanitary paperwork was in order.

The ship date was February 4th. I live somewhere that, on February 4th, is reliably eighteen degrees Fahrenheit. Luxurians comes from a place that is reliably seventy-five and wet. Heat packs help; they do not perform miracles inside a USPS truck idling at a regional sort facility in Ohio.

I asked the grower to hold until April. He couldn't — the export window was the export window. I let it go. The plant I would have received in February would have been a tube of mush by the time I opened the box, and the email I would have sent afterward would have been worse for both of us than the email I sent instead, which said: next season.

What the pause is actually for

I don't think any of this makes me a careful collector. I have bought plants I shouldn't have. I have a Monstera in a corner that cost more than it should have and is, three years on, a perfectly ordinary Monstera. The pause doesn't make you immune. It just makes you slightly less likely to be the protagonist of someone else's anecdote about a scam.

The thing the pause is for, I've come to believe, is not protecting your money. It's protecting your relationship to the hobby. Every plant you buy under hypnosis — at one in the morning, on a phone, from a stranger, against a backdrop of black velvet — is a plant you'll later have to look at and remember being hypnotized. The pause lets the plant arrive as a plant rather than as a souvenir of a feeling.

So I keep the notes file. I paste in the wire instructions. I leave the tab open overnight. About half the time I wake up and the plant has lost whatever it had at midnight, and I close the window without regret. The other half, the wanting survives daylight, and I send the money, and the box arrives, and the plant is what it said it was. Those are the ones that go on the shelf by the window. The almost-bought live somewhere else — in a notes file, in this essay, in the small private museum of restraint that every collector eventually builds whether they meant to or not.

Rare plants, real stories — a few times a week.
