Rainforest: Sumatra, Indonesia — Giants and Corpse Flowers

In Sumatra's lowland dipterocarp and peat-swamp forest, plants didn't just grow — they grew to extremes, and every record-holder is a clue to what extreme competition does over geological time.

The smell hits before the sight does. A sweetish, gagging wave — rotting meat, old fish, something metabolically wrong — rolling out from a riverbank thicket of Macaranga scrub in the Barisan foothills of Sumatra. Then you see it: a single, mottled, burgundy-and-cream spathe rising three metres out of the ground, its edges crimped like a ruff, its spadix a finger of pale cream pointing straight up into the canopy gap. Amorphophallus titanum in full bloom lasts thirty-six to forty-eight hours. If you are standing in front of one in the wild, you are extremely lucky and should understand that this event, this spike, this theatrical rotting smell that pollinating carrion beetles can detect from kilometres away, was seven years in preparation underground.

The forest behind it is not dramatic in the usual way. There is no single view, no grand savannah panorama. Sundaland's lowland dipterocarp rainforest closes around you in layers — a dim, dripping understorey forty metres below a canopy that barely lets through five percent of available light. The competition here is not between predator and prey but between photons and root space, between a strangler fig's roots and the trunk they're devouring, between a Rafflesia embryo threading itself through a host vine and the vine's immune response trying to stop it. The forest has been running this competition for perhaps sixty million years without a hard frost to reset the board. What it produced is the most biologically extreme flowering-plant community on the planet.

Common questions

Where does Amorphophallus titanum grow in the wild?
Amorphophallus titanum is endemic to the equatorial rainforest of Sumatra, Indonesia, particularly the Barisan mountain foothills in Bengkulu and surrounding provinces. It grows in lowland and submontane forest edges and disturbed areas where canopy gaps allow some direct light. Wild populations are increasingly fragmented due to deforestation.
Can Rafflesia arnoldii be grown in cultivation?
No established method exists for cultivating Rafflesia arnoldii outside its natural habitat. It is a holoparasite entirely dependent on the root and stem tissue of specific Tetrastigma vines, and its integration with host tissue cannot be replicated artificially. Some botanic gardens have produced flowers by transplanting infected host material, but no self-sustaining ex situ population exists.
What Hoya species are native to Sumatra?
Sumatra hosts numerous Hoya species, including Hoya latifolia and several undescribed or recently described taxa. The genus is exceptionally diverse across Sundaland, occupying different elevations and host trees as epiphytic climbers. Many Sumatran hoyas remain poorly collected and are known only from limited herbarium material.
Why is Sumatra's lowland rainforest being destroyed so quickly?
Lowland dipterocarp and peat-swamp forest in Sumatra occupies flat, accessible terrain that is easily converted to palm oil (Elaeis guineensis) plantations. Peat swamp areas are drained and burned, releasing enormous quantities of stored carbon. Sumatra has lost more than half its original forest cover since the mid-1980s, with Riau province particularly severely affected.
