The Same Plant, Three Prices: What You're Actually Paying For

In one week, a Philodendron spiritus-sancti cutting sold for $80, $240, and $900 — and none of the buyers were wrong.

On a Tuesday in March, a two-node cutting of Philodendron spiritus-sancti closed on a Facebook auction for $82, including shipping from Indonesia. On Thursday, what looked like the same plant — same long, drooping juvenile leaves, same etiolated stems — sold on a US grower's Instagram story for $240. On Saturday a specimen with four leaves, two of them adult, went out the door of a Florida nursery for $900 and a waitlist position for the next available cut.

Three transactions, one species, an order of magnitude in price. Collectors argue about this constantly, usually in the comments under someone else's haul post. The honest answer is that all three prices are defensible. The dishonest answer — the one that drives the loudest arguments — is that any of them is the real price.

What a price actually contains

A plant price is a bundle. Inside it: the cost of the cutting itself, the cost of getting it legally across a border, the cost of the months it sat under someone's lights acclimating, the seller's risk that it dies in transit, and the seller's reputation, which functions as insurance. Strip those apart and the spread between $82 and $900 stops looking like gouging and starts looking like an itemized invoice nobody bothered to itemize.

The Indonesian export at $82 is a fresh cut, often unrooted, sometimes with the cambium still weeping. It traveled in newspaper and a plastic sleeve, cleared phytosanitary inspection on the exporter's certificate, and arrived with the buyer absorbing every downstream risk: rot at the base, thrips eggs in the petiole sheath, a month or two of sphagnum nursing before the plant decides whether it wants to live.

The $240 plant is the same genetics six months later, rooted in pon or chunky aroid mix, hardened off to ambient humidity, and photographed under daylight-balanced bulbs by someone whose DMs you can find. You are paying $158 for time, attrition, and the fact that someone else ate the import gamble.

The $900 specimen is a different object entirely. Four leaves means the plant has demonstrated it will keep producing leaves. Two adult leaves means you can see the species express itself — the elongated, pendulous blades that distinguish spiritus-sancti from every photograph people mistake for it. You are buying a finished plant and, more importantly, a plant that has stopped being a coin flip.

Price in this hobby is not a number attached to a species. It is a number attached to a story, and the story is what you're buying.
