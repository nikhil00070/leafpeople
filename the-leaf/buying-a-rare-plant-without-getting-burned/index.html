How to Vet a Seller and a Plant Before You Pay

Before the wire transfer clears, there are a dozen quiet ways to tell whether the cutting in the photo is the cutting you'll actually receive.

The DM arrives at 11:47 on a Tuesday night: a Philodendron spiritus-sancti, well-rooted, four leaves, $850, shipping Wednesday if you can send today. The photo is sharp enough to count the trichomes on the petiole. The seller's feed shows a tidy grow tent, a few satisfied buyers, a repost from someone you vaguely recognize. Your thumb hovers over the payment app. This is the moment the hobby tests you.

Vetting is not paranoia. It is the price of entry to a market that runs on cuttings, trust, and international postage, where the same cultivar name can mean three different plants and a juvenile leaf can look like almost anything. The collectors who have been at this for a decade are not smarter than you. They are slower. They ask one more question, request one more photo, wait one more day. The rest of this piece is what those questions look like.

Read the feed, not the post

A single listing tells you almost nothing. The feed around it tells you most of what you need to know. Scroll back twelve months. Look at how the same mother plant has been photographed across seasons — a real Anthurium magnificum in someone's collection will show up repeatedly, with new leaves emerging on the same growth point, in the same pot, against the same wall. Sellers who flip rapidly often post a plant once, sell it, and never reference it again. That is not a deal-breaker, but it is a pattern.

Watch for the slow accumulation of competence: pictures of root systems, captions that mention substrate ratios, the occasional dead leaf left in frame. Hobbyists who actually grow tend to document growing. Resellers tend to document inventory. Both can be legitimate. Only one is in a position to tell you why a particular Philodendron mcdowell has been sulking since it arrived from the Philippines.

Cross-reference. If a seller claims a plant came from a specific grower — say, Equatorial Exotics or a known Thai nursery — that grower's own feed often shows the same flat of seedlings months earlier. Provenance leaves a paper trail when it is real.

A good seller will not be insulted by a careful buyer. A bad seller will be, and that itself is information.

Ask for the photo you weren't given

Every listing photo is curated. What is missing is the interesting part. Ask for the underside of the newest leaf, the petiole sheath, the back of the pot, the root ball, and a picture taken today with a piece of paper showing your username and the date. This last one feels theatrical until the first time it saves you from buying a stock photo.

For velvet species — verrucosum, melanochrysum, gigas, luxurians — the petiole is the tell. Pubescent or smooth, the angle of the cataphyll, the shape of the geniculum. A juvenile gloriosum and a juvenile pastazanum look similar in a thumbnail and entirely different when you can see how the petiole meets the blade. If the seller cannot or will not provide that angle, assume there is a reason.

For Anthurium, ask about the parentage if it is a hybrid, and the seed source if it is a species. "crystallinum x papillilaminum" means nothing without knowing whose cross. The same nominal hybrid from two different breeders can produce wildly different plants. A seller who shrugs at this question is selling you a name, not a plant.

The price tells a story

Markets price information. When a plant is suspiciously cheap, the seller usually knows something you do not. A $200 Philodendron joepii in 2024 is either mislabeled, virused, declining, or — occasionally — a generous deal from someone exiting the hobby. Ask which. The honest answers are interesting; the evasive ones are diagnostic.

Suspiciously expensive is its own warning. Hype cycles inflate certain cultivars — the Monstera albo market has done this repeatedly — and the people pricing at the top of the curve are often the last to have bought in. Check completed sales, not asking prices. Aroid auction groups and a few of the larger Discord servers keep informal records. If a plant has sold three times in the last month at half the asking price, the asking price is fiction.

There is also the matter of the cutting versus the established plant. A node with one leaf is not worth eighty percent of what a four-leaf rooted specimen costs, regardless of what the seller insists about the mother. Pay for what is in the box.

Inspect for the things that do not show up in photos

Thrips damage looks like silvery stippling on the leaf surface and tends to concentrate near the midrib. Spider mites leave fine webbing in leaf axils and the undersides of new growth. Mealybugs hide in cataphylls and root crowns. None of these are death sentences, but all of them are now your problem, and they will become your other plants' problem within a week if you do not quarantine aggressively.

Ask directly: has this plant been treated for pests, and with what, and when. A seller who has used systemic imidacloprid in the last month should tell you, because it changes how you handle the plant on arrival. A seller who claims their entire collection is pest-free is either lying or has not looked closely enough. Everyone has thrips eventually.

Viruses are harder. Dasheen mosaic shows as chlorotic streaking and distorted new growth in Philodendron and Anthurium, but it can be latent for months. There is no cure, only culling. If a plant's new leaves are emerging with strange mottling that does not resolve as they harden, do not buy it, and do not accept the explanation that it is "just stress."

Shipping is part of the plant

A beautifully grown Anthurium forgetii packed badly in January will arrive as a stick. Ask how the seller ships: heat packs or not, insulation type, how the root ball is secured, whether the plant is shipped bare-root or in substrate, and what carrier. Priority Mail is two to three days domestically in the US; anything longer in winter is a gamble even with a 72-hour heat pack.

International shipping adds CITES paperwork, phytosanitary certificates, and customs. A seller offering to ship a rare species across borders without phytos is offering to get your plant seized and possibly to get you on a list. The savings are not worth it. Reputable importers — and there are a handful of good ones in the US, Europe, and Southeast Asia — handle the paperwork and price it in.

Agree in advance on what happens if the plant arrives damaged. "DOA policy: photos within 24 hours of delivery, partial refund or replacement" is standard and fair. "No refunds once shipped" on a $600 plant is a seller telling you they do not stand behind their packing. Believe them.

The references nobody asks for

The aroid world is smaller than it looks. Three or four messages to the right people will tell you whether a seller has a history of mislabeling, ghosting, or shipping declining plants. The big Facebook groups are unreliable for this — drama distorts everything — but private Discords and the older forums still function as reputation systems. Ask a collector you trust whether they have bought from this person. Ask twice if the answer is vague.

Pay attention to who follows whom. When established growers and importers follow a seller back, it is usually because that seller has earned it through years of clean transactions. When a seller's followers are mostly new accounts and bot-like profiles, treat the social proof as the marketing it is.

None of this is foolproof. People with sterling reputations have bad months; new sellers can be excellent. But the base rate of fraud in a market this lucrative and this unregulated is not zero, and the cost of one careful hour is much less than the cost of one $1,200 mistake.

When to walk away

Walk away when the seller pressures you to decide tonight. Walk away when the answers to specific questions get vaguer rather than more specific. Walk away when the only payment option is friends-and-family on a peer-to-peer app with no recourse. Walk away when the photos are too perfect, or when they are the same three photos that appeared on someone else's listing last spring. Walk away when your gut, trained by a few hundred hours of looking at plants, tells you the leaf in the picture is not the species on the label.

The plant you wanted will appear again. spiritus-sancti is not as rare as it was a decade ago; neither is verrucosum, neither is the white-veined Anthurium you have been chasing. Tissue culture, seed-grown imports, and a wider grower base have flattened most of the truly impossible wants into merely expensive ones. Patience compounds. The collector with a six-year-old, perfectly grown luxurians paid less, in total, than the one who bought and killed three of them in eighteen months.

Vetting is, in the end, an act of respect — for the plant, for the grower who raised it, for the version of yourself who has to keep it alive next February. Slow down. Ask the boring question. The cutting will still be there in the morning, or it won't, and either answer is useful.

Rare plants, real stories — a few times a week.
