Botanical Tour: Kew's Palm House & Princess of Wales Conservatory, London, UK — Where Glasshouse Botany Was Born

Two glasshouses at the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew contain more botanical history per square metre than almost anywhere else on Earth — and the plants inside are the ancestors of everything you grow.

You smell it before you see it. Push through the heavy glass door of the Palm House at Kew on a cold London morning and a wall of warm, saturated air hits you — the particular perfume of damp bark, decomposing leaf litter, and something green and faintly ferrous that the body reads, on some animal level, as jungle. The iron ribs arch overhead like the hull of a ship turned inside out. Condensation runs down the curved glass. A Livistona palm pushes its fan fronds against the ceiling seventy years after it outgrew any reasonable pot. For a collector who keeps a velvet philodendron alive under a grow light in a spare bedroom, this place is a provocation and a comfort in equal measure.

The Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2003, a recognition less of its 132 hectares of landscaped grounds than of what those grounds represent: the hub through which the botanical wealth of the tropical world was collected, classified, and in many cases redistributed into cultivation. Kew named plants. Kew sent collectors into mountain cloud forests and river systems that had never produced a herbarium sheet. The glasshouses are where that project became physical — where a living palm from southern Africa or a cycad dug up in 1775 was given a home and kept alive through English winters by coal, cast iron, and a great deal of institutional determination.

Common questions

When is Kew Gardens open and how do I get there?
The Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew is open daily year-round, though opening and closing hours vary by season. The nearest tube station is Kew Gardens on the District line. Tickets must be purchased in advance online or at the gate, and entry includes access to the glasshouses.
Why is the Palm House at Kew famous?
The Palm House, built between 1844 and 1848, is considered the world's most important surviving Victorian glass-and-iron structure. It was designed by Decimus Burton and engineered by Richard Turner, whose curved iron hull design allowed a large tropical glasshouse to be built without obstructing internal columns. It has housed a living tropical collection continuously since the mid-nineteenth century.
What is the Titan arum and can I see it at Kew?
Amorphophallus titanum, known as the Titan arum or corpse flower, is one of the largest flowering structures in the plant kingdom. Kew grows specimens in the Princess of Wales Conservatory and has flowered several over the years. Blooms are unpredictable; Kew announces them on its website and social media channels as they occur.
What is the oldest plant at Kew?
The Eastern Cape giant cycad, Encephalartos altensteinii, growing in the south wing of the Palm House, was collected in South Africa in 1775 and has been in Kew's care ever since. It is widely cited as the oldest pot plant in the world, making it one of the most historically significant living specimens in any botanical garden.
