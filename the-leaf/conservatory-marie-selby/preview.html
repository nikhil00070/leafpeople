Botanical Tour: Marie Selby Botanical Gardens, Sarasota, USA — The World Capital of Epiphytes

On a bayfront estate in Sarasota, the world's only epiphyte-focused botanical garden holds the institutional heart of everything collectors grow.

The banyan tree at Marie Selby Botanical Gardens does not ask permission to exist. Its aerial roots drop from branches like a slow argument with gravity, touch the Florida soil, and thicken into secondary trunks until the tree has colonized enough ground to shelter a small crowd. Bromeliads roost in its crotches. Ferns colonize the bark. It is, without any curatorial intervention, an illustration of the garden's entire thesis: that the most complex and competitive real estate in a tropical ecosystem is vertical, not horizontal, and that the plants worth knowing grow in the air.

Selby, as regulars call it, opened to the public in 1975 on the bayfront estate that Marie Selby, a Sarasota civic figure and plant enthusiast, bequeathed upon her death. The property sits at the edge of Sarasota Bay, where the light comes off the water and bounces up into the canopy at angles that make the collection glow on a clear morning. It is a serious research institution — there is a herbarium here, a Bromeliad Identification Center, and a scientific staff that publishes — but it is also genuinely beautiful in the way that only happens when expertise and place coincide. For collectors of rare aroids, orchids, and epiphytic bromeliads, a visit is less a sightseeing trip than a pilgrimage to the institutional home of the plants on their grow racks.

Common questions

What are Marie Selby Botanical Gardens' hours and admission?
Hours and admission prices change seasonally, so check the official Selby Gardens website at selby.org before visiting. The garden is generally open daily, with extended hours during special events and exhibitions. Member discounts are available.
What makes Selby different from other botanical gardens?
Marie Selby Botanical Gardens is the only botanical garden in the world with a primary scientific and curatorial focus on epiphytes — plants that grow on other plants rather than in soil. This includes orchids, bromeliads, aroids, gesneriads, and ferns. The garden maintains a research herbarium and the Bromeliad Identification Center, making it a working scientific institution as well as a public garden.
What should I not miss on a first visit to Selby?
The Tropical Conservatory is the centerpiece of the collection and the best place to see epiphytes in dense, climate-controlled conditions. The banyan tree on the grounds and the orchid and bromeliad displays are also essential. The bay views along the grounds give the property its distinctive character.
Is Selby Gardens worth visiting if I grow aroids and rare houseplants?
Yes — Selby's collection is directly relevant to collectors of aroids, orchids, hoyas, and bromeliads, since these are predominantly epiphytic plants. Seeing mature specimens in well-maintained conditions recalibrates expectations for size, habit, and mounting technique in ways that photographs and home growing cannot replicate.
