The Cult of the Cup Spathe

The anthuriums nobody at the party recognizes are the ones worth knowing.

There is a particular moment in an anthurium collection when the obvious purchases stop feeling like enough. You have your crystallinum, leaves wide as dinner plates with silver venation that catches the window light. You have a magnificum with its quadrangular petiole and the faint ridges running down each side. Maybe a warocqueanum hangs from a basket in the corner, doing that drooping, theatrical thing. The plants are extraordinary. They are also, by now, extremely recognizable — photographed ten thousand times, sold at every major auction, hashtagged to exhaustion. You know their names before you see the label.

Then someone sends you a photo: a chalice-shaped spathe, the limb folded inward at the margins, cupping the spadix like a hand shielding a flame from the wind. No flat reflexed bract, no flare. Something compact, odd, almost architectural. You ask what it is. The answer is Anthurium crystallinum x dressleri? No — something else. Anthurium ravenii, maybe. Anthurium eminens. Anthurium crenatum. You look them up and find almost nothing. That absence is the beginning of a different kind of obsession.

What the Cup Spathe Actually Is

The term is informal — anthuriums are not formally classified by spathe shape in a way that maps cleanly onto collector shorthand — but it describes something real. In a typical aroid-collector's anthurium, the spathe is reflexed: it opens flat or sweeps back behind the spadix, acting like a billboard. In cup-spathe species, the limb curves inward or holds its margins upright, partially enclosing the base of the spadix. The effect is less announcement, more enclosure. Some species sit at the extreme end, the spathe nearly tubular; others are just cupped enough to look structurally strange beside a conventional bloom.

Anthurium ravenii, collected in Panama and Costa Rica, is a reasonable starting point for understanding the type. Its spathe is green, waxy, and deeply cupped, the margins never reflexing fully, the whole structure sitting close around the base of a pale yellow spadix. It is not decorative in any obvious way. It looks, to the uninitiated, like a plant that hasn't finished blooming yet. That confusion is almost the whole point.

Several species in section Porphyrochitonium show this tendency, as do members of section Belolonchium and a handful of climbing species whose taxonomy taxonomists are still arguing about. What they share is a spathe that was never designed for display — at least not for human display. The enclosure protects the spadix through the pistillate phase, manipulates beetle and fly movement within the floral chamber, and may regulate the microclimate around developing pollen. Form follows function, and the function has nothing to do with what looks good in a collection.

The cup spathe doesn't photograph well, which is exactly the point — it rewards the person standing in front of it.

Why Collectors Come to Them Late

There is a loose but reliable sequence to how people move through anthurium collecting. Velvets first: crystallinum, clarinervium, forgetii with its windowed sinus. Then the elongated foliage types — warocqueanum, papillilaminum, the ongoing argument about whether your dark form is really regale or just a large magnificum grown in low light. Then a move toward species that are harder to find, harder to keep, harder to identify: dressleri (true dressleri, not a hybrid sold as one), besseae and its forms, villenaorum before it was widely tissue-cultured.

Cup-spathe species tend to appear at step four or five, when the collector has already exhausted the more photographable obsessions and starts asking what the genus actually contains beyond what appears in sale groups. The answer is several hundred described species, many of which have never been offered commercially at all, and a subset whose appeal requires standing in front of them rather than scrolling past a photo. That's a meaningful filter. The plants self-select for people who find the absence of hype interesting rather than off-putting.

There's also a practical dimension: cup-spathe species rarely command the auction prices of velvet flagships. Anthurium eminens, a robust climbing species with a distinctive cupped green spathe, changes hands for a fraction of what a well-variegated warocqueanum costs. That price gap doesn't reflect any deficit in the plant — it reflects the fact that cup-spathe species don't perform on Instagram, and so the speculative premium never accumulated.

Species Worth Knowing by Name

Anthurium crenatum is probably the most widely cultivated of the true cup-spathe types, though it still sits well outside mainstream collection. Native to the Caribbean islands — Puerto Rico, Hispaniola, parts of the Lesser Antilles — it produces a spathe with slightly crenulated margins and a waxy, almost succulent texture. The blade stays cupped through flowering and doesn't reflex even as the plant ages. It tolerates slightly lower humidity than most collector anthuriums, which makes it useful as well as interesting.

Anthurium ravenii rewards patience more than most. It grows slowly, climbs when given a moss pole, and the foliage — elongated, matte green with a faint corrugation — is unremarkable until you've looked at it long enough to see the proportion. The spathe appears only after the plant is well-established, and it's green enough that you might miss the first one if you're not watching. Once you do see it: the cupped structure, the cream spadix, the way the whole thing looks like a compressed flower still deciding what to do — it lodges somewhere.

Anthurium eminens is a larger, more vigorous plant, appropriate for collectors with space to spare or a grow tent that's already running warm and humid. The leaves are elongated-cordate, the petiole terete, and the spathe — green, reflexed only partially, cupping at the base — sits at the end of a long peduncle in a way that's architecturally satisfying if you're paying attention. Less commonly discussed is Anthurium obtusum, a smaller-statured species from Ecuador and Colombia with a distinctly cupped spathe and leaves that remain relatively compact, making it workable in a smaller setup.

Growing Them: The Actual Care

The care requirements for most cup-spathe species track closely with the broader anthurium playbook, with some adjustments. Drainage is non-negotiable: a mix of orchid bark, perlite, and a small amount of coco coir, or a chunky peat-free blend with good air pockets throughout. The roots of these species — many of which are hemiepiphytic climbers in habitat — suffocate in dense media faster than you'd expect. The medium should feel barely moist through most of the active growing period, dry enough at the surface between waterings to maintain oxygen in the root zone.

Humidity above 65% keeps the foliage in good condition, though Anthurium crenatum will tolerate 55% without immediate complaint. Temperatures between 65°F and 85°F cover the range; avoid sustained cold below 58°F for any of the Neotropical species. Bright indirect light — the kind you'd give a crystallinum — works well, though climbing species appreciate the ability to orient toward a light source rather than sitting static in one position.

Fertilizing at quarter-strength with a balanced formula every two to three weeks during active growth keeps them moving without salt accumulation. Flush the medium monthly if you're feeding regularly. The one consistent mistake with slower-growing cup-spathe species is impatience: they are not magnificum, which can push a new leaf every few weeks in good conditions. Some of these plants will give you six or seven leaves a year and that is fine. The growth pattern is part of what they are.

The Provenance Problem

One of the difficulties with obscure anthurium species is identification confidence. The plants in collector hands often arrived through informal channels — cuttings brought back from botanical garden visits, divisions passed between growers, material that predates widespread tissue culture by decades. Labels get separated from plants. Common names drift. A 'cupped green anthurium' in someone's collection might be ravenii, might be a species-adjacent hybrid, might be something undescribed.

This isn't unique to cup-spathe anthuriums, but the problem is more acute because there is less photographic reference and fewer people who have grown the plants long enough to observe the full range of variation. Thomas Croat's work at the Missouri Botanical Garden, which has produced hundreds of anthurium species descriptions over the past several decades, is the standard taxonomic reference, but the gap between herbarium specimens and living cultivated plants remains wide. An inflorescence in alcohol tells you less than watching a plant bloom across two seasons.

The practical advice is to source from collectors who have grown the plant long enough to see it bloom, who can provide a reliable chain of provenance, and who are honest about uncertainty. 'Likely eminens, possibly an undescribed species from the same region' is more trustworthy than a confident label on a plant that arrived as a cutting of a cutting of a cutting. In a part of the hobby where the documentation is thin, epistemic humility is a feature, not a weakness.

What the Cup Spathe Is Actually For

Return, for a moment, to that structural strangeness — the inward-curving limb, the enclosure around the base of the spadix. The anthurium inflorescence is a rewarder-and-deceiver system: it produces heat, volatile compounds, and sometimes visual signals to attract specific pollinators during the pistillate phase, then shifts chemistry during the staminate phase to encourage the now-pollen-laden visitor to leave and find another inflorescence. The cupped spathe modifies this system by restricting access to the floral chamber, making the environment inside warmer, more saturated with volatiles, and more physically constrictive for small beetles and flies.

This is not an ornamental decision. The spathe shape evolved in dialogue with specific insects in specific forests at specific elevations, over timescales that make the collector's appreciation of it somewhat beside the point. What it means for the collector is that the cup is a record: of a place, an elevation, a guild of pollinators that probably have no common name in English and may not have been formally described. Growing Anthurium ravenii in a grow tent in Ohio is a kind of translation — approximate, inevitably lossy, still worth doing.

The cup spathe doesn't photograph well in the way that a silver-veined velvet leaf does. The depth of the structure collapses in flat light; the green-on-green tonality confuses phone cameras. That difficulty is, from a certain angle, exactly what recommends it to the collector who is tired of growing plants for photographs. The plant rewards the person standing in front of it with good light and enough context to understand what they're seeing. That's a narrower audience, and it suits these plants well.

The Far End of the Rabbit Hole

Every collection eventually arrives at a question about what you're actually doing — what it means to keep a Panamanian hemiepiphyte alive through a Pittsburgh winter, why the flowering of an obscure species from an herbarium specimen matters to you at all. Cup-spathe anthuriums tend to surface this question more forcefully than the velvets, because the aesthetic payoff is so much more conditional. You have to know the plant to see what it's offering.

That conditionality is what keeps the far end of the rabbit hole interesting. There will always be a new crystallinum form or a whiter warocqueanum making rounds in the auction groups. The cup-spathe species sit mostly outside that economy. They are grown by people who find the absence of recognition clarifying — who have decided that the point of the collection is the collection, not the photograph of it.

If that describes you, Anthurium crenatum is a reasonable way to start the conversation. Give it a well-draining mix, consistent warmth, humidity you'd be comfortable providing any anthurium, and enough patience to watch it develop on its own schedule. When it blooms — and the spathe curves inward around the spadix in that distinctly private way — you will understand something about the genus that the velvet phase didn't teach you. That's enough of a reason.
