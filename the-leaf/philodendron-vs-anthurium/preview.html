Philodendron vs. Anthurium: How to Tell Them Apart

Philodendron and Anthurium borrow each other's best tricks — velvet, white veins, heart-shaped leaves — but three features tell them apart every time, and none of them is the leaf you're staring at.

Stand at a good collector's bench and you will see the problem. A dark, heart-shaped, velvety leaf laced with chalk-white veins sits in one pot. Two pots over, a dark, heart-shaped, velvety leaf laced with chalk-white veins sits in another. They could be sisters. One is a Philodendron and one is an Anthurium, and they are not even close relatives — no more alike, botanically, than a shark and a dolphin, two animals that arrived at the same streamlined shape by swimming down the same evolutionary river from opposite banks.

That resemblance is a genuine problem and not just a party trick, because the two genera are worth different money, want different care, and get mislabeled constantly — sometimes by honest accident, sometimes not. The good news is that you never have to guess. Ignore the part of the plant that fools everyone, the leaf, and look instead at three features that give an honest answer every time: a joint, a sheath, and, if you are lucky enough to catch one, a flower that is not a flower.

Common questions

What is the easiest way to tell a Philodendron from an Anthurium?
Feel the top of the leaf stalk, just below the leaf. Anthuriums have a geniculum there — a swollen, bendable joint like a little knee that lets the leaf pivot toward light — and Philodendrons do not. Knee present means Anthurium; a smooth stalk with no knee means Philodendron. It is the single most reliable check and takes seconds.
Why do Philodendron and Anthurium look so similar if they aren't closely related?
They belong to the same plant family (Araceae, the aroids) but to separate genera, and their resemblance is mostly convergence — two unrelated plants evolving the same features because they live the same way, in the dim, humid understory of American rainforests. Velvet leaves, pale veins, and heart shapes are shared solutions, not shared ancestry. That is why the leaf itself is the least reliable thing to judge by.
How can I tell them apart from the new growth?
Philodendrons protect each new leaf with a cataphyll, a papery sheath that dries to brown tissue and sheds, so an actively growing philodendron is usually littered with crispy brown envelope-like cast-offs around its newest leaves. Anthuriums don't produce those conspicuous shedding sheaths. A crowd of dried papery sheaths points to Philodendron; clean emergence points to Anthurium.
Does the flower really tell you which genus it is?
Yes, definitively, if you ever get one. Anthurium blooms carry bisexual flowers with tiny tepals on a spadix backed by a flat, open spathe, and the spadix often ripens into colorful berries. Philodendron blooms have tepal-less, unisexual flowers on a zoned spadix wrapped in a hood-like spathe, and many even heat up at night to spread scent. The catch is that neither blooms often indoors, so the knee and the sheath are the everyday tests.
