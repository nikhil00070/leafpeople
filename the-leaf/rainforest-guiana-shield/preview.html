Rainforest: The Guiana Shield (Venezuela & the Guianas) — The Lost World of the Tepuis

On the Guiana Shield, two-billion-year-old stone and millions of years of isolation produced a flora so strange it convinced a novelist that dinosaurs still walked the earth.

Stand at the base of Roraima on the Venezuelan-Guyanese border and the cliff above you is vertical for three hundred meters. The sandstone is Precambrian — laid down before complex animal life existed — and the summit is invisible in cloud. What lives up there, you are told, does not live anywhere else. Not a variety. Not a closely related species. The actual thing, found on this one flat-topped mountain and on no other surface of the earth.

The Guiana Shield is a craton, a chunk of ancient continental crust that has been sitting more or less where it sits now for roughly two billion years. It spans six countries — Venezuela, Guyana, Suriname, French Guiana, northern Brazil, and a tongue of Colombia — and from its surface the tepuis rise like broken teeth, sheer-walled tabletop mountains shaped by the slow dissolution of the Roraima Formation sandstone. The rivers that drain the shield run black with tannins and carry almost no dissolved minerals. The soils, where they exist at all, are white sand and quartzite: sterile, acid, chronically starved of phosphorus and nitrogen. It is a landscape that has been torturing plants into ingenuity for longer than most of the world's mountain ranges have existed.

Common questions

Where does Philodendron billietiae come from?
Philodendron billietiae was formally described from French Guiana in 1998 and is native to lowland forests of the Guiana Shield region, including parts of French Guiana and adjacent northern Brazil. It typically grows as a hemiepiphyte along forest edges and riverine corridors in warm, humid lowland forest.
What are tepuis and why do they have so many endemic species?
Tepuis are sheer-walled, flat-topped sandstone mountains rising from the Guiana Shield in Venezuela, Guyana, Suriname, and adjacent countries. Their vertical cliffs isolate summit populations from one another as effectively as oceanic islands, allowing plant and animal populations to diverge independently over millions of years. An estimated one-third to two-thirds of the plant species on any given tepui summit are found there and nowhere else on earth.
Why does the Guiana Shield have so many carnivorous plants?
The shield's ancient Precambrian soils are chronically deficient in phosphorus and nitrogen, leaving plants with almost no mineral nutrition available from the substrate. Carnivory — trapping insects to digest their proteins and phosphates — evolved independently multiple times in these conditions as an alternative nutrient source. The sun pitcher genus Heliamphora, endemic to the tepuis, includes roughly twenty-three species each adapted to particular summits.
How should I care for Philodendron atabapoense?
Philodendron atabapoense evolved along blackwater rivers with near-zero mineral content, extremely acid conditions, and very fast-draining substrates. Grow it in a chunky, airy mix of orchid bark, perlite, and a small amount of sphagnum, keep humidity between 65–80% with good airflow, and fertilize sparingly with a low-phosphorus formula. Avoid compacted or moisture-retentive mixes, which recreate nothing from the plant's native habitat and will promote root rot.
