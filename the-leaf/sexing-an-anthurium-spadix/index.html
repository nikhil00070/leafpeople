Reading the Spadix

An anthurium bloom is a slow-burning clock, and learning to read it is the difference between seed and silence.

The spadix on my Anthurium forgetii has been pale green for nine days. This morning it wears a faint sheen, almost waxy, and when I lean close there is a smell I can only describe as cold melon rind. A bead of clear liquid sits at the base of one of the tiny tepals — not condensation, not guttation. Stigmatic fluid. The plant is open for business, and it will not say so twice.

Anthurium flowers are not flowers in the way most houseplant growers mean the word. The colorful spathe is a bract, a flag. The real action happens on the spadix, that finger of densely packed perfect flowers, each one cycling through a female phase and a male phase on a schedule the plant keeps to itself. If you want hybrids — if you want to cross your luxurians to your dressleri and see what falls out — you have to learn to read that finger. Miss the window by a day and you are pollinating a closed door.

What the spadix actually is

Each bump on the spadix is a complete, hermaphroditic flower — four tepals, four stamens, a single gynoecium with two or three locules. On a mature Anthurium andraeanum inflorescence you might count six hundred of these crammed onto a single column the size of your little finger. They do not all bloom at once. A wave of receptivity travels up the spadix over days, and a second wave — the male phase — follows behind it, usually after a deliberate gap.

This sequence is called protogyny. Female first, then male, with the plant going to some trouble to keep the two phases from overlapping on the same inflorescence. It is the genus's main defense against selfing, and it is the single most important fact for anyone who wants to make seed. You are not waiting for the bloom to open. You are waiting for two distinct phases on two different inflorescences, ideally on two different plants, to line up.

The spathe is theater. The spadix is the contract, and it is written in a language of sheen, scent, and a single bead of fluid.

The pistillate phase: a quiet shine

Female receptivity is easy to miss because nothing looks like it is happening. The spadix has finished elongating. The spathe has reflexed or held its position. The color is whatever the species wears at maturity — pale green on forgetii, ivory on warocqueanum, a dusty rose on some crystallinum clones — but the surface picks up a wet, lacquered quality. Under a loupe you can see the stigmas as tiny raised dots, each crowned with a microdroplet.

That droplet is the tell. Stigmatic fluid is sugary, slightly viscous, and it appears in a band that creeps up the spadix from the base over three to seven days. Touch the spadix gently with a clean fingertip and the fingertip should come away tacky. If it comes away dry, you are early or late. If it comes away wet enough to bead, the plant is at peak receptivity and you have perhaps forty-eight hours.

Scent helps. Many anthuriums emit a faint floral or fruity volatile only during the pistillate phase — bubblegum on some antioquiense, anise on certain papillilaminum, the cold-melon note I get from forgetii. The scent vanishes when the female window closes. If you walked past the plant yesterday and smelled something and today you do not, you have just learned the timing of that clone.

The staminate phase: pollen, finally

After the female wave passes, the spadix goes quiet for anywhere from a few days to several weeks. This intermission is not failure. It is the plant resetting. Then the stamens begin to emerge — four per flower, pushing out between the tepals in a second wave that climbs the spadix the same direction the first one did.

Pollen on most anthuriums is white to pale cream, sometimes faintly yellow, and it comes out wet. Fresh pollen looks like tiny grains of cooked rice studded along the column. Tap the spadix over a piece of dark paper and you should see a dusting. If nothing falls, wait twelve hours and try again; pollen presentation is often diurnal, peaking in late morning under good light.

Collect into a clean gel cap or a folded square of glassine. Label it — species, clone, date — and refrigerate at around 4°C if you cannot use it immediately. Anthurium pollen stores surprisingly well: viable for weeks refrigerated, and for months to years if you can manage a home freezer at -20°C with desiccant. Serious hybridizers keep a pollen library the way some people keep a spice rack.

Making the cross

The mechanical act is almost anticlimactic. A small artist's brush, size 0 or 1, sable if you have it. Dip into the collected pollen, then paint it onto the receptive spadix in a slow spiral from base to tip. Some growers prefer a fingertip; the oils on human skin do not seem to bother the stigmas, and you get better tactile feedback about whether the surface is still tacky.

Do it twice, twenty-four hours apart, to catch flowers that came into receptivity overnight. Then label the inflorescence at the peduncle with the cross written in pencil on a strip of blind tag: pod parent × pollen parent, date. Pencil survives humidity. Sharpie does not.

If the cross takes, you will know within two to three weeks. The spadix begins to swell unevenly, fertilized flowers bulging while unfertilized ones flatten and brown. Over the next four to twelve months — yes, months; A. warocqueanum can take nearly a year — the spadix transforms into a club of berries, each containing one or two seeds embedded in mucilaginous pulp.

Why timing fails

The most common reason a cross does not take is not technique. It is that the grower pollinated a spadix that looked ready but was not, or was ready three days ago. Anthuriums in cultivation often bloom out of sync with each other, especially across species lines, and the windows are narrow.

This is why stored pollen matters so much. If you are trying to cross a luxurians that throws one inflorescence a year against a dressleri that blooms in a different month, you cannot rely on simultaneity. You bank pollen from whichever plant sheds first and wait, sometimes for the better part of a year, for the other to enter pistillate phase.

Humidity also matters. Stigmatic fluid evaporates fast below 60% RH, and the receptive window collapses with it. Growers in dry climates sometimes bag the inflorescence loosely with a clear plastic sleeve during the female phase, or pollinate inside a closed grow tent in the morning when humidity peaks.

A word on selfing and sibling crosses

Protogyny makes selfing hard but not impossible. If you have a single clone and want seed from it, you store pollen from one inflorescence and apply it to the next inflorescence's pistillate phase weeks or months later. Some species self readily this way; others, particularly the section Cardiolonchium velvets, are stubbornly self-incompatible and will drop everything regardless of how perfect your timing is.

Sibling crosses — two seedlings from the same pod, pollinated to each other — are the workhorse of anthurium breeding and the reason so many named hybrids share genetics in ways their labels do not reveal. If you are buying seed-grown plants and the vendor cannot tell you the pod and pollen parents by name, assume nothing about what the seedling will look like at maturity. The spadix keeps its own counsel, and so should you.

Common questions

How long is an anthurium's female phase receptive?
Typically three to seven days, with a peak window of roughly forty-eight hours when stigmatic fluid is visibly beaded on the spadix. The exact length varies by species and by ambient humidity — drier air shortens the window considerably. Check the spadix morning and evening once the spathe has fully opened.
Can I use pollen from the same plant to pollinate its own bloom?
Sometimes, but not from the same inflorescence. Anthuriums are protogynous, meaning the female phase finishes before the same spadix sheds pollen, so you must store pollen from one inflorescence and apply it to a later one. Some species are also self-incompatible and will reject their own pollen regardless of timing.
How do I store anthurium pollen?
Collect dry pollen into a labeled gel capsule or glassine fold and refrigerate at around 4°C for short-term use, up to several weeks. For longer storage, freeze at -20°C with a silica desiccant packet; viability of months to a few years is reasonable under those conditions. Always let the container reach room temperature before opening, to prevent condensation.
How long until anthurium berries ripen after a successful cross?
Four to twelve months, depending on the species. Anthurium andraeanum hybrids often ripen in four to six months, while velvet species like warocqueanum and papillilaminum can take eight to twelve. Berries are ripe when they swell, soften, and shift color — usually to red, orange, white, or purple depending on the species — and begin to push free of the spadix.
