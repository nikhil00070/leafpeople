Genus Basics: The Hoya

Five hundred species of climbing, dripping, star-flowered vines that are not aroids at all — and the one cut you must never make.

Most people meet a Hoya before they know its name. It's the thick-leaved vine on a grandmother's windowsill, the one that seems immortal and occasionally, without warning, hangs out a cluster of tiny flowers so precise they look machined. The leaves feel like they've been dipped in wax, which is exactly why the common name is wax plant. For years it sat in the background of houseplant culture. Then collectors noticed how many different Hoyas there were, and the background became a hobby of its own.

Leaf People files Hoyas next to the aroids — the Monsteras and Philodendrons and Anthuriums — because the same people tend to grow both. But it's worth being clear from the start: a Hoya is not an aroid. It belongs to a completely different branch of the plant family tree, and that difference explains almost everything about how it behaves.

Common questions

Are Hoyas the same as Monsteras and other aroids?
No. Hoyas belong to the dogbane and milkweed family (Apocynaceae), while Monsteras and Philodendrons are aroids in a completely separate family. Collectors grow them side by side because both are tropical climbers, but botanically they're unrelated. The practical upshot: Hoyas want to dry out far more than most aroids do.
Why won't my Hoya flower?
Almost always it's a shortage of light, a lack of maturity, or a cut spur. Hoyas need bright light to bloom and often have to grow up and fill their pot first. And if you've been trimming off the bare woody stalks after flowers fade, you've been removing the exact spots where next year's flowers would form.
My Hoya's flowers are dripping sticky liquid. Is something wrong?
No, that's normal and a sign of a happy plant. Many Hoyas produce sweet nectar that beads up and drips from the flowers, sometimes called sweating. It's how they attract night pollinators. Just set a saucer underneath heavy bloomers to catch the drops.
What's the best Hoya for a beginner?
Start with a common, cheap one: plain Hoya carnosa, a silver-splashed pubicalyx, or the round-leaved kerrii. All three tolerate irregular care and are hard to kill. Learn the dry-out watering rhythm on one of these before investing in thin-leaved or heavily veined species that are less forgiving.
