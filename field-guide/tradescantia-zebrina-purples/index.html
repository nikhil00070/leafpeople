Field Guide · Tradescantia

Tradescantia zebrina and the Easy Purples

Tradescantia
4–6 hrs Direct sun for full pigment

The color is genetic, but the saturation is on you — light, stress, and substrate decide whether your zebrina glows or fades.

The picks
01
Tradescantia zebrina 'Burgundy'
Trailing · broad striped leaf

The benchmark deep form: leaves nearly black-purple on the reverse, with two wide silver bands above that darken to violet in strong light. Holds color far better than generic zebrina when grown bright. Stems thicken quickly and root from any node tossed in water. The cultivar most likely to deliver the saturated look people picture when they say 'wandering dude'.

Top pick
02
Tradescantia zebrina 'Discolor Multicolor'
Trailing · variegated sport

A chimeric form streaked pink, cream, green, and purple — no two cuttings identical. Color is unstable and reverts to plain zebrina if light drops or you keep cutting from a green-dominant stem. Propagate only from the most variegated nodes and accept that some will revert anyway. A patience plant, not a beginner one despite the genus.

For collectors
03
Tradescantia pallida 'Purpurea'
Upright trailer · linear leaf

Often sold as Setcreasea or purple heart. Solid magenta-purple stems and lance-shaped leaves with no silver banding — the cleanest block of color in the genus. Needs direct sun for that color; in shade it goes muddy green-purple with leggy internodes. Hardy outdoors to zone 8 and indestructible in a bright window.

Best for sun
04
Tradescantia fluminensis 'Nanouk'
Compact trailer · fleshy leaf

A patented sport of T. fluminensis with thick, stacked leaves striped pink, white, and green. Slower and tidier than zebrina, which makes it the better choice for a small shelf. Pink pigment depends on light — keep it just below where the leaves would scorch. Drops lower leaves fast if overwatered.

Most compact
05
Tradescantia spathacea 'Tricolor'
Rosette · stiff strap leaf

Moses-in-the-cradle, the upright cousin. Rigid pink-cream-purple striped leaves in a tight rosette rather than a vine. Tolerates more neglect than the trailing species and rarely gets leggy. A useful counterweight on a shelf full of trailing purples.

Architectural

Why color fades: light, stress, and the truth about anthocyanins

The purple in Tradescantia is anthocyanin pigment, and the plant produces it as a sunscreen. That single fact explains almost every washed-out specimen on the internet. In low light, the plant has no reason to spend energy on pigment, so it shifts toward chlorophyll green and the silver bands of zebrina go pale, the reverse turns lavender instead of wine.

Give a zebrina four to six hours of direct sun — an unobstructed east window, a south window behind a sheer, or a strong grow light at 200–400 µmol — and the color deepens within two or three weeks. T. pallida wants even more; full midday sun outdoors is where it earns its name. If you cannot provide that light, buy a green-leaved trailer and stop fighting the plant.

Mild stress sharpens color further. Slightly cooler nights (down into the low 60s F), letting the substrate dry to nearly bone between waterings, and going easy on nitrogen all push the plant to make more anthocyanin. Lush, fast, well-fed growth is paler growth. This is the rare houseplant where benign neglect produces a better-looking specimen than attentive care.

Substrate, water, and the rot problem nobody warns you about

Tradescantia stems are essentially water balloons with leaves. They store enough moisture to coast through a missed watering, which is why they wilt dramatically and then bounce back an hour after a drink. The corollary is that they rot at the base if kept consistently wet, especially in dim light. More zebrina dies from kindness than from drought.

A working mix: two parts peat- or coir-based potting soil, one part perlite, one part orchid bark or pumice. The bark is the part most people skip and the part that matters — it keeps air at the root zone where the lowest, oldest stems sit on the soil surface. Terracotta helps. A shallow pot helps more; these are not deep-rooted plants and a tall pot just holds cold wet soil under the roots.

Water when the top inch is dry and the pot feels light. In summer with good light that might be twice a week; in winter, once every ten days or longer. Humidity is genuinely optional — these plants come from disturbed ground in Mexico and Central America, not cloud forest, and 40% room humidity is fine. Airflow matters more than moisture in the air; a stagnant shelf invites the gray fuzzy stem rot that ends a plant overnight.

Pruning, propagation, and common mistakes

The single biggest mistake is letting the plant grow long and never cutting it back. Tradescantia shed lower leaves as stems extend, so an untouched plant becomes a bald rope with a tuft at the end. Pinch every stem back by a third every couple of months. Each cut triggers branching at the node below, and the plant fills in dense rather than stringy.

Root the cuttings — do not throw them out. Three to five nodes stripped of their lowest leaves, stuck directly into the parent pot or a glass of water, will root in under a week. This is how you keep a pot looking full: continuous replacement of old growth with new. After about two years even a well-maintained plant gets woody and tired at the base, and the honest move is to propagate a full pot of cuttings and compost the original.

Other failures worth naming. Sunburn shows as bleached patches on the upper leaf surface; move the plant back six inches and it recovers. Spider mites love dry, hot Tradescantia — check the leaf undersides monthly, especially in winter heat. Variegated forms reverting to plain green or plain purple means light is too low or you are cutting from the wrong stems; prune out the reverted growth aggressively or it will dominate. And resist fertilizing more than monthly at quarter strength. A hungry zebrina is a purple zebrina.
