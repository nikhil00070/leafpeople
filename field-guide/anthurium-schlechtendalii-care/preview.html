Anthurium schlechtendalii, the Giant Bird's-Nest Anthurium

Anthurium
6 ft Leaves can exceed this length in mature specimens

The largest of the bird's-nest anthuriums grows leaves longer than a person is tall and asks for surprisingly little in return.

Light
Bright indirect, no direct midday sun
Water
Top inch dry between thorough waterings

Anthurium schlechtendalii
Terrestrial · bird's-nest rosette

The headline plant: a Central American giant ranging from southern Mexico through Panama, often growing terrestrially on limestone or epiphytically on big branches. Mature rosettes hold strap-shaped leaves three to six feet long arranged in a tight funnel that catches leaf litter. The inflorescence is unshowy — a green spathe and a purple-tinged spadix that ripens to magenta berries. It is the most forgiving large anthurium you can grow and the one most likely to actually fit through your doorway after a year.

Common questions

How do I tell Anthurium schlechtendalii from Anthurium hookeri?
Check the leaf margins and the berry color. A. hookeri has tiny black resin glands dotted along the leaf edge and produces white berries, while A. schlechtendalii has clean margins and magenta-to-purple berries. Mature schlechtendalii also grows substantially larger, with leaves routinely exceeding four feet.
Is Anthurium schlechtendalii toxic to pets?
Yes — like all anthuriums it contains calcium oxalate crystals, which cause oral irritation, drooling, and swelling if chewed. The thick, tough leaves are less inviting than a softer houseplant, but cats and dogs that chew should be kept away. Sap can also irritate human skin on prolonged contact.
How fast does it grow, and how big will it actually get indoors?
Expect one to three new leaves per growing season indoors, each larger than the last until the plant reaches its ceiling for your light and pot size. In a bright room with a 12–14 inch pot, a young plant typically reaches a four- to five-foot spread within three to four years. Truly wild-sized specimens with six-foot leaves require greenhouse conditions or an atrium.
Why are the older outer leaves yellowing and dying?
Some loss of the oldest leaves is normal — a bird's-nest anthurium sheds outer foliage as it pushes new leaves from the center. Concerning yellowing is uniform across multiple leaves at once and usually means overwatering, compacted media, or root damage. Unpot, check for soft brown roots, and repot into fresh chunky mix if more than a few have failed.
Does it need to be mounted, or is a pot fine?
A pot is fine and is what almost all cultivated plants live in. While schlechtendalii grows epiphytically on large branches in the wild, its mature weight and water needs make mounting impractical outside a greenhouse. A deep pot with a chunky, airy mix replicates the wild root environment well enough.
