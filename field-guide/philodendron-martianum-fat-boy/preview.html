Philodendron martianum 'Fat Boy' and Its Swollen Petioles

Philodendron
40 cm Typical mature leaf length on a well-fed martianum

The bulbous, water-storing petioles of 'Fat Boy' make it one of the strangest terrestrial philodendrons you can grow — and one of the most rewarding.

Light
Bright indirect, 1,500–2,500 fc; no direct sun
Water
Top 3–4 cm dry between waterings; reduce in winter
Humidity
60–75%; struggles noticeably below 50%
Native range
Atlantic Forest, southeastern Brazil
Mature size
Rosette to ~60 cm wide; leaves to 40 cm
Difficulty
Intermediate; unforgiving of wet, compacted substrate
The picks
01
Philodendron martianum 'Fat Boy'
Terrestrial · non-climbing · swollen petiole

The cultivar name refers to the dramatically inflated petioles — spongy, pale-green cylinders that function as water reservoirs and give the plant its unmistakable silhouette. Leaves are glossy, dark green, and broadly elliptic, held nearly upright from the rosette. Growth is slow but steady when given warmth and a loose, nutrient-rich substrate. Because it doesn't climb, it suits a wide, shallow pot rather than a moss pole.

Common questions

Why are Philodendron martianum 'Fat Boy' petioles so swollen?
The inflated petioles function as water-storage organs, an adaptation to the seasonally variable moisture of the Atlantic Forest floor. They allow the plant to buffer short dry periods without immediate leaf stress. This is an exaggerated version of the modest petiole thickening seen in the straight species — 'Fat Boy' represents a selected form where the trait is most pronounced.
Is Philodendron martianum 'Fat Boy' a climber?
No — martianum is a terrestrial, non-climbing philodendron that grows as a compact rosette rather than vining upward. It produces no aerial roots for attaching to supports and will not benefit from a moss pole. A wide, shallow pot suits its spreading root system better than a tall standard container.
How do I tell if my martianum is overwatered versus just dropping old leaves?
Overwatering typically shows as simultaneous yellowing across several leaves, soft dark lesions at the petiole base, and a substrate that smells sour. Normal senescence is a single outer leaf yellowing while the plant is actively pushing new growth from the centre. If in doubt, check the petioles: firm and turgid means the plant is adequately hydrated; soft, sunken spots at the base of a petiole indicate rot beginning.
What substrate is best for Philodendron martianum?
A chunky, free-draining mix that still retains moderate moisture works best — roughly equal parts quality potting mix, perlite or pumice, and orchid bark, with a small amount of worm castings for nutrition. Avoid peat-heavy mixes that compact over time and exclude oxygen from the root zone. Standard potting soil used alone is the most common substrate mistake with this species.
Can Philodendron martianum 'Fat Boy' tolerate lower humidity?
It will survive at 50% humidity but growth slows, leaf size decreases, and margins may brown. Consistent performance requires 60% or above. Among the terrestrial philodendrons, it is somewhat less humidity-tolerant than gloriosum but roughly comparable to plowmanii — if your space runs dry, mamei or plowmanii are more forgiving choices.
