Anthurium pentaphyllum and the Strange Case of the Compound Leaf

Anthurium
5–11 Leaflets per mature leaf

The one anthurium that refuses to grow a simple leaf — and how to keep it climbing.

Light
Bright indirect, 200–400 PPFD, no direct sun
Water
Top inch barely damp; never soggy
Humidity
70%+ ideal, tolerates 55% with airflow
Substrate
Chunky aroid mix; bark, perlite, light sphagnum
Support
Moist sphagnum pole required for mature leaves
Native range
S. Mexico to northern South America, lowland forest
Mature size
Climbing to 6+ ft; leaves 12–18 in across

Anthurium pentaphyllum
Hemiepiphytic climber · palmately compound

The headliner, and the only widely grown Anthurium in section Schizoplacium with reliably palmate-compound leaves. Mature plants throw five to eleven leaflets radiating from a single petiole, looking more like a Schefflera than an anthurium until the inflorescence appears. Native from southern Mexico through northern South America, usually climbing small trees in humid lowland forest. Juvenile leaves are simple or trifoliate, so patience is part of the deal.

Common questions

Why does my Anthurium pentaphyllum only produce simple leaves?
It is still in its juvenile phase and almost certainly not climbing. Pentaphyllum only develops palmate compound leaves once it attaches aerial roots to a moist support and reaches a certain stem maturity. Mount it on a damp sphagnum pole, keep the moss wet, and expect the transition over the next several leaf cycles.
Is Anthurium pentaphyllum the same as Anthurium polyschistum?
No, they are separate species in section Schizoplacium, though they are routinely confused in trade. Polyschistum is smaller overall with narrower, more numerous leaflets and a thinner stem, while pentaphyllum has broader leaflets and a heavier climbing habit. Mature inflorescences and spadix color help confirm ID.
How long until Anthurium pentaphyllum matures?
Roughly one to three years from a rooted juvenile, depending on light, humidity, and whether it has a proper climbing support. Plants without a pole can stay juvenile indefinitely. Once mature, each new leaf typically shows more leaflets than the last, plateauing at five to eleven.
Can Anthurium pentaphyllum be grown without a pole?
Technically yes, practically no. Without a support to climb, the plant remains juvenile and the leaves stay simple or trifoliate, which defeats the point of growing the species. A sphagnum or tree-fern pole kept consistently moist is the difference between a mediocre houseplant and the canopy-style specimen the species is known for.
Is it toxic?
Yes, like all anthuriums it contains calcium oxalate crystals in the sap and tissue. Chewing leaves or petioles causes immediate oral irritation in pets and humans. Keep it out of reach of cats, dogs, and small children, and wash your hands after pruning.
