Field Guide · Amorphophallus

Amorphophallus at Home: Growing the Bloom Giants Without Losing the Corm

Amorphophallus
6–9 Months of dormancy per year

The carrion lilies are easier than their reputation suggests — until the leaf collapses and you have to keep a sleeping corm alive in the dark.

The picks
01
Amorphophallus konjac
Terrestrial · single annual leaf

The standard starter corm, and still the one most worth growing. A mature konjac throws a dappled, umbrella-like leaf on a serpent-patterned petiole that can clear five feet, then — given enough corm mass — a maroon spathe that smells like roadkill for about thirty-six hours. Tolerant of ordinary potting practice and forgiving of dormancy errors that would kill rarer species. Bulks up fast: a 200 g corm can double in a single season under good feeding.

Best starter
02
Amorphophallus bulbifer
Terrestrial · bulbil-producing leaf

Native to the eastern Himalayas and famously generous: it forms small bulbils along the leaf rachis, so one corm becomes a colony within a few years. The inflorescence is pink-flushed and only mildly offensive as aroid stinks go. Habit is compact compared to konjac, which makes it the most apartment-friendly species in the genus. Excellent for collectors who want to trade or gift offsets.

Most generous
03
Amorphophallus titanum
Terrestrial · giant single leaf

The titan arum — a tree-sized leaf on a single spotted petiole, and eventually, after seven to ten years and a corm the weight of a toddler, the largest unbranched inflorescence on earth. Realistic only if you have a tall, warm, humid space and the patience to feed a sleeping basketball for a decade. Seedlings are widely available and surprisingly cheap; the cost is square footage and time. Not a windowsill plant, but not impossible either.

Ambitious project
04
Amorphophallus paeoniifolius
Terrestrial · edible food crop

The elephant foot yam, grown across South and Southeast Asia as a staple. The inflorescence is a ruffled, cabbage-like spathe in liver-red and cream, broader than tall, and unmistakable. Leaves are heavily divided and dramatic, and the corm bulks faster than almost any other species — half-kilo gains in a season are routine. Worth growing for the bloom alone, which lasts longer than most Amorphophallus flowers.

Fastest grower
05
Amorphophallus kiusianus
Terrestrial · cold-hardy corm

A Japanese species that handles cooler conditions than most of the genus and can be grown outdoors in mild temperate gardens with mulched winter protection. The leaf is finely divided and elegant, and the small purple-black spathe is among the prettier blooms in the genus. A good choice for collectors in zone 7–8 who want to keep a corm in the ground rather than chase pots in and out of the garage.

Cold tolerant

Substrate and pot choice

Amorphophallus corms rot from the top down, almost always because water sat in the depression where the bud emerges. Build a mix that drains fast and holds nutrients without staying wet: roughly two parts coarse bark or coco husk chunks, one part pumice or perlite, one part good quality peat or coir, and a generous handful of worm castings. Skip moisture-retentive amendments like sphagnum on the surface — you want the crown to dry between waterings even in active growth.

Pot size matters more than people expect. A corm that filled a 6-inch pot last year may need a 12-inch this year; they grow downward and outward, and a cramped pot produces a smaller daughter corm. Choose deep over wide — most species send a contractile root system well below the corm. Terracotta is fine if you water attentively; plastic is safer if you tend to forget. Always pot with the bud pointing up and the top of the corm level with or just below the soil surface, never buried deep.

Light, water, and humidity in active growth

Once the leaf emerges — and it emerges fast, sometimes inches per day — treat the plant like a hungry tropical understory species. Bright indirect light is the target: an east window, a lightly shaded south, or a foot or two back from a grow light putting out 200–400 µmol. Direct midday sun will scorch the photosynthetic petiole, which is the leaf for the season; lose it and the corm loses the year.

Water deeply when the top inch of mix dries, and feed every two weeks with a balanced liquid fertilizer at half strength. The leaf is doing one job: building next year's corm. Humidity above 50% keeps leaflet edges from crisping, but airflow matters more than absolute humidity — stagnant warm air invites fungal spots on the petiole. Outdoor summers in dappled shade produce the best growth I've seen; the plants want heat, light, and a real breeze.

Dormancy: where most corms die

Sometime in autumn the leaf yellows and collapses, often within a week. This is normal and non-negotiable — every Amorphophallus is dormant for six to nine months a year, and trying to force continuous growth will exhaust and kill the corm. When the petiole detaches with a gentle tug, stop watering completely.

You have two options. The lazy method: leave the corm in its dry pot somewhere cool (55–65°F) and dark, and check it monthly. The careful method, which I prefer for anything rare or expensive: lift the corm, brush off the old mix, inspect for soft spots, dust any cuts with cinnamon or sulfur, weigh it, and store it in a paper bag of dry vermiculite or coco coir in a closet. Label everything. A corm that gains weight from one dormancy to the next is a happy corm; one that loses more than 15% is telling you something went wrong in the previous season.

Restart in spring when you see the bud swell and color up — usually a pink or burgundy nub at the crown. Repot into fresh mix, water once lightly, and then wait until the shoot has cleared the soil before watering again in earnest. The first watering after dormancy is the single most common cause of rot in this genus. Patience here is the whole game.

Common mistakes

The classic errors, in order of frequency: watering a dormant corm because the pot looks dry; potting too shallow so the crown collects water; using a dense peat-heavy mix that stays sodden; panicking when the leaf drops in autumn and trying to revive it; and underestimating how much pot a mature corm needs. None of these are subtle, and all of them are avoidable once you accept that this is a seasonal plant, not an evergreen.

One more: if your corm blooms, it will not produce a leaf that same year. The inflorescence draws down the entire stored reserve, and the corm needs the following season to rebuild. Feed heavily the year after a bloom and expect a smaller leaf — that's the plant working, not failing.
