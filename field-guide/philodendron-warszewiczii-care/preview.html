Philodendron warszewiczii: The Dissected Dry-Season Dormant

Philodendron
60+ cm blade length at maturity

A deeply lobed giant that sheds leaves in the dry season and rewards collectors patient enough to wait it out.

Light
Bright indirect; 200–300 PPFD; no harsh afternoon sun
Water
Drench and dry; reduce by half during winter dormancy
Humidity
55–70%; tolerates brief dips; prioritize airflow
Difficulty
Intermediate — dormancy cycle needs understanding
Native range
Mexico to Costa Rica; seasonally dry tropical forest
Mature size
Blades to 60+ cm; terrestrial spread to 1.2 m across
The picks
01
Philodendron warszewiczii
Terrestrial · dissected leaf

Native to Central America from Mexico down through Costa Rica, warszewiczii produces paddle-shaped juvenile leaves that transform into dramatically pinnatifid adult blades — each lobe long, narrow, and slightly wavy. In the dry season it may shed most of its foliage, pulling resources into a stout, tuberous-ish base before re-flushing when humidity and moisture return. Mature specimens can push blades over 60 cm with 10 or more pairs of lateral lobes. It is terrestrial in habit, so it does not need a pole and actually resents being forced to climb.

Common questions

Why is my Philodendron warszewiczii dropping all its leaves?
Leaf drop in autumn and winter is normal dormancy behavior for warszewiczii, not a sign of disease or care failure. The plant evolved in seasonally dry Central American forests and responds to shorter days and cooler temperatures by shedding foliage and conserving resources in its base. Reduce watering frequency significantly and wait — new growth will emerge in late winter or spring when conditions improve.
Does Philodendron warszewiczii need a moss pole or climbing support?
No — warszewiczii is a terrestrial species, not a hemiepiphyte, and performs best without a climbing support. Forcing it upward does not produce larger leaves and can stress the plant by orienting it against its natural growth habit. Give it a wide, shallow pot or a ground-level position in a warm greenhouse and let it spread laterally.
What substrate works best for Philodendron warszewiczii?
A fast-draining mix is essential: roughly two parts coarse perlite or pumice, one part medium orchid bark, and one part coir works well. Avoid peat-heavy or moisture-retentive mixes, which stay wet in the center of the pot long after the surface dries — a condition that leads to basal rot in a plant adapted to dry periods. Terracotta pots help by wicking excess moisture and signaling dryness visually.
How is Philodendron warszewiczii different from Philodendron bipinnatifidum?
Both are large, terrestrial, pinnatifid philodendrons, but bipinnatifidum (now reclassified as Thaumatophyllum bipinnatifidum) tends to grow larger overall, with blades regularly exceeding 90 cm, and develops a distinctive corky exterior on mature stems. Warszewiczii stays somewhat smaller, has a more pronounced seasonal dormancy, and originates from Central America rather than South America. Bipinnatifidum also tolerates a wider range of humidity and light conditions, making it slightly easier for beginners.
When should I fertilize Philodendron warszewiczii?
Fertilize only during active growth, typically late spring through early autumn. A dilute balanced fertilizer in a roughly 3-1-2 NPK ratio applied at half the recommended rate every three to four weeks supports healthy leaf expansion without pushing soft, vulnerable growth. Stop fertilizing entirely when the plant begins shedding leaves for dormancy — adding nutrients to a plant entering dormancy does not accelerate recovery and can encourage weak, pest-prone tissue.
