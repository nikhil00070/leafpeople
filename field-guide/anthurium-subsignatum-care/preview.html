Anthurium subsignatum: Care and Form

Anthurium
7–11 Lobes on a mature leaf

A Central American climber with deeply lobed leaves, a quiet temperament, and a habit of being mistaken for its more famous cousins.

Light
Bright indirect, no direct sun
Water
Top inch dry, core damp; rainwater preferred
Humidity
65%+ ideal, struggles below 50%
Substrate
Chunky aroid mix on a damp pole
Native range
Costa Rica and Panama, wet lowland forest
Mature size
Leaves 40–60 cm across on a climbing specimen
The picks
01
Anthurium subsignatum
Hemiepiphytic climber · matte green, lobed

The subject of this guide. Native to Costa Rica and Panama, it pushes out leaves that mature from a simple sagittate blade into a deeply pedately-lobed form with seven to eleven finger-like segments. The texture is matte, not velvet, and the petioles are long and wiry, built for scrambling up bark in wet lowland forest. A patient grower — the lobed adult form only appears once the plant commits to a totem.

Common questions

How do I tell Anthurium subsignatum from Anthurium pedatoradiatum?
Mature subsignatum has seven to eleven lobes with a longer central segment and narrower sinuses, while pedatoradiatum produces larger leaves with more numerous, more deeply cut lobes and a flatter overall silhouette. Juveniles are nearly impossible to separate by leaf alone. Ask the seller for a photo of the parent plant's adult foliage before paying a rare-plant price.
Why are my Anthurium subsignatum leaves still heart-shaped and not lobed?
The pedate lobing is an adult trait that appears only once the plant is actively climbing and producing longer petioles. Pot it against a damp sphagnum or tree-fern pole, keep humidity above 60%, and feed lightly through the growing season. Lobing typically begins three to five leaves after the plant commits aerial roots to the support.
Does Anthurium subsignatum need a terrarium?
No, but it does best in an enclosed cabinet or a humid grow room. It will live in open room conditions at 50% humidity with crisp edges and slow growth, and it thrives at 70%+ with airflow. A fully sealed terrarium is actually risky because the species is prone to bacterial blight in stagnant, saturated air.
Is Anthurium subsignatum rare or expensive?
It is uncommon in general retail but well-established in specialist collections, so prices have come down considerably from the 2021 peak. Expect to pay mid-range rare-aroid money for a seed-grown juvenile and more for a climbing specimen with adult leaves. Wild-collected plants still appear on the market and should be avoided on both ethical and acclimation grounds.
